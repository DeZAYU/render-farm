# The Scary & Disturbing Amusement Parks Iceberg Explained

## INTRO

This is the scary and disturbing amusement parks iceberg. Near the top, it's malfunctions guests caught on their phones, like a launch cord fraying in Florida, where everyone got off alive. Each level down gets heavier, until we reach a small French park where one coaster took two lives. Viewer discretion is highly advised.

## LEVEL 1 — THE TIP

Level one is the tip. These are the ones people filmed or still argue about online, and nobody in them died.

### CASE 1 — THE VERTICAL ACCELERATOR CORD

Krissy Hurst had ridden the Vertical Accelerator plenty of times, but on a Saturday night in August 2019 she stayed on the ground and filmed it. The ride's own camera wasn't working, which is why she was filming at all.

The ride stood at Cobra Adventure Park in Panama City Beach, Florida. It's a reverse bungee, which works like a bungee jump played backwards. Two huge rubber cords run from a steel cage up to two towers. The cage is held down on the deck while the cords stretch tight, and then it's let go and flung into the sky.

Nick and Vic are strapped in for their first ride. Hurst's friend was meant to go too, but pulled out at the last second over a bad feeling. I'm glad she listened to it.

The cords are pulled tight, and the attendant in the yellow shirt walks over to the launch controls.

Then the left cord bursts.

Neither man was hurt. One of them said later he was in total shock, his hands still stuck up in the air, with no idea what had just happened.

The park took both men off and refunded them, though Hurst said nobody there seemed very sorry. The manager said they ran safety checks every day, and the ride stayed open.

Florida's ride inspectors said they'd check whether the cord had been maintained properly and whether it had gone past its allowed number of uses. I'd love to know how many launches it had done. Whatever the state found was never made public.

### CASE 2 — SAW MILL LOG FLUME

A father from Long Island is standing near the end of the Saw Mill Log Flume at Six Flags Great Adventure in New Jersey, waiting for his daughters and nieces to come around. It's a gray Sunday evening in June 2021, and his wife is filming.

A log flume is a water ride where a fake log carries you down a channel and over drops. Six Flags advertised this one, opened in 1974, as the world's first. It wasn't. The first was El Aserradero in 1963, at Six Flags Over Texas, so the company lost that record to itself.

Their log comes down the last drop and swings into the bend by the old mill house. Watch the wall on that bend, where the spray comes over. A piece of guide rail there is out of place. They just got quite a jolt, and the family only spotted the rail when they watched this back. It's easy to miss in all that spray.

Less than a minute later, the next boat hit the wall right there. That part isn't on camera. It rode up and stopped, tipped at what one witness put at about 45 degrees, but stayed upright. Two riders went to the hospital with leg and arm pain.

Six Flags said the boat didn't complete its cycle and shut the ride for inspection. The father said it could've been his family. Would you put them back on it? He said he would, and he'd ride it himself. I respect that.

The flume reopened a month later with the rail repaired. It closed again in 2024 for a refurbishment and came back in 2026, more than fifty years after it first opened.

### CASE 3 — EL GUSANITO

El Gusanito is a little worm train coaster you'll find at fairs all over Mexico, built for kids and their parents. The one in this clip is packed with grown-ups.

It's night, and the ride is looping a low oval under blue and white lights. A grinning green head in a cartoon hat pulls a chain of round cars, with the riders squeezed in two to a car. I've got no idea how some of them folded themselves in.

Keep your eye on the far side of the loop.

Whoever posted this on February 22, 2016 wrote that the worm split in two. That's true, but it isn't a break down the middle. The head and its first car pull away and run on alone, while everyone else falls back.

When the train stops in the station, a man in a striped sweater, apparently the operator, walks along the cars. And the first thing he checks? The worm's head. One of the most-liked comments caught that before the passengers did. The riders climb out over the sides, and some of them are laughing.

These little rides aren't always harmless. In Guatemala in 2017, a different worm ride lost its last car and a rider was badly hurt.

No injuries were ever reported, and nothing official ever turned up, though hundreds of commenters have asked. This clip has been viewed more than three million times, and nobody can say where it was filmed.

### CASE 4 — FUTUROSCOPE OBJECTIF MARS FIRE

On April 7, 2023, at 10:16 in the morning, a coaster train at Futuroscope in France was finishing its run with a fire underneath it.

Futuroscope, outside Poitiers, spent most of its life as a park of screens and simulators. In 2020 it finally got a real roller coaster, Objectif Mars, a spinning coaster that cost 22 million euros, the park's biggest investment since it opened in 1987. Riders are meant to be training for Mars, in cars that spin freely as they launch around roughly 1,600 feet of track.

The fire started in a lithium battery under a seat on the second car. I'd never once thought about what's under a coaster seat.

This was filmed from the queue, behind the yellow railing. Watch the car as it comes in.

Then the smoke hits.

The train couldn't stop out on the track, so it had rolled in with everyone still strapped in. One witness said there was screaming everywhere and nobody really knew what to do. The riders had to climb out of their cars into that smoke. Staff grabbed extinguishers, and so did a man who'd only been waiting in line. I hope somebody thanked him.

The fire was out within minutes, and they'd cleared the station by about 10:20. Two women from that car had light burns, and one went to the hospital in Poitiers for observation. The park said the ride had been checked every day and maintained the way the manufacturer recommended, and it closed until further notice.

It reopened on June 23, 2023, with the batteries taken out and the spinning switched off, though the park never said why that battery caught fire. The cars didn't spin again until 2025.

### CASE 5 — THE ULTIMATE AND THE DEER

Picture yourself in the front row of The Ultimate at Lightwater Valley in North Yorkshire, England. When it opened in 1991, this steel coaster was the longest in the world, about 1.4 miles of track. A ride lasts around seven and a half minutes, and most of it runs out through the woods, far from the crowds.

It's about a quarter past eleven on a Saturday morning in late September 2014, and your train's coming around at speed.

There's a young deer standing on the track.

The train hit it, and the deer was killed instantly. None of the riders were hurt, though a few came back into the station badly needing a wash. Staff closed the ride for about half an hour, cleaned up and then opened it again.

A park spokeswoman said a big perimeter fence surrounds the park, but now and then wild animals get in overnight. And it wasn't the first time. In June 1994, another deer wandered out of the woods and onto the same track. Twice in twenty years? That's two more than most coasters ever manage.

The Ultimate ran for five more seasons and didn't open in 2020 because of the pandemic. In January 2023 the park's new owners started tearing it down over the cost of meeting safety standards, and by April the old record holder was gone.

### CASE 6 — DUELING DRAGONS

At Universal's Islands of Adventure in Orlando, two roller coasters were built to look like they were about to crash into each other. On Dueling Dragons, two trains left the station together, riders' legs dangling under the track, and they twisted around each other so they'd pass within feet at up to 60 miles an hour. In 2010 it became Dragon Challenge for the Wizarding World of Harry Potter, but the duel didn't change. That near miss was why you got in line.

On July 31, 2011, Carlos Montalvo, a 52-year-old nurse visiting from Puerto Rico, was on board. Just as the trains swept past each other, something hit him in the right eye, and he didn't see what it was. His lawyer later said the eye had to be removed, and Montalvo's other eye was already weak.

Universal reopened the ride in less than a day. In its injury report to the state, the park called what happened to him "eye discomfort." I can't think of a milder way to put losing an eye.

What hit him? It was never found. About two weeks later, another rider said something struck him in the face at the same spot, and again nothing turned up. The likeliest answer is a loose phone or keys flying from one train into the other, but it's never been confirmed.

Universal said the ride wasn't to blame, but it put up signs about lockers and started launching the trains apart. In October 2011 the duel ended for good.

I get why people loved it. But if you sell two trains passing that close, anything that comes loose from one has the other right there to hit.

### CASE 7 — SON OF BEAST

When Son of Beast opened at Kings Island in Mason, Ohio, in April 2000, no wooden roller coaster on Earth had ever been bigger. It stood 218 feet tall and hit 78 miles an hour, and it had a 118-foot vertical loop that no other wooden coaster had. That's a lot to ask of timber, isn't it?

Trouble started almost right away. The park had fired the builder before construction was even finished, and I'd have had questions right there. Within weeks it shut so a rough stretch of track could be smoothed, and complaints about how hard it hit people didn't stop.

Then came the afternoon of July 9, 2006. A train went into the Rose Bowl, a big wooden helix late in the ride, where a support under the track had failed and left a dip. The train slammed straight through it. All 27 people on board were taken to the hospital, none with life-threatening injuries.

Ohio inspectors gave their answer that December. The wooden supports had never been designed to carry the ride's weight, and one vertical timber, called a bent leg, had cracked under the load.

The park's fix was lighter trains. They couldn't handle the loop, so the loop came out, and in July 2007 Son of Beast came back as just another tall wooden coaster. I think that's when it stopped being special.

It didn't last. In June 2009 a woman told the park she'd had a burst vessel in her brain after a ride, and Kings Island closed it that day. The state found nothing wrong with the ride, but it never ran again.

The lift hill came down in November 2012, and Banshee, a steel coaster, opened on the same ground in 2014.

## LEVEL 2 — BELOW THE SURFACE

Below the surface, rides start failing in front of crowds, and this is where the first deaths show up.

### CASE 8 — QUEENS LAND FREE FALL TOWER

Two platforms full of riders are sitting near the top of a red tower, and the one on the right has a board on it that says Free Fall. This is Queens Land, an amusement park on the highway west of Chennai, India, on the afternoon of June 18, 2019. A free-fall tower is about as simple as thrill rides get: you're hauled up, here about 150 feet, and dropped.

There are 24 people aboard, 12 on each side. The left platform drops first, and that's the ride working normally. Now watch the right-hand one on its way back down.

It's nearly home, about ten feet up.

The cables had snapped.

If it had happened at the top, one officer said, all 12 would have died. Instead the riders had minor injuries, and after first aid on the spot they were treated at a nearby hospital and sent home.

The police were angrier about what happened next. The park didn't report the crash, and police say it talked the riders into a compromise. Nobody filed a complaint, and no police case was ever reported.

Officers say they only found out from this video, which by Friday was everywhere. Without this clip, I'm not sure we'd ever have heard about it. In the assistant commissioner's words, the park tried to hide the whole thing. Police had written to the park that April after complaints about its rides, and he said it claimed to have checked everything, which clearly wasn't the case. The park refused to comment.

On June 21, police ordered the whole park shut indefinitely, until it could show a fitness certificate for every ride.

### CASE 9 — ALBANIFEST TOP SPIN

Every June since 1971, the Swiss city of Winterthur has thrown the Albanifest, a street festival that draws about a hundred thousand people. Late one Saturday night in 2009, someone's phone is pointed up at its Top Spin, a long gondola hung between two big arms that swings riders high and flips them head over heels.

Just before half past midnight, in the middle of a ride and without slowing down at all, it stops dead.

Look at the gondola now. It's frozen above the neon sign with the arms level, and 28 people are sitting in it upright, a good 16 feet off the ground, with the lights still flashing around them. I'd be counting every minute up there.

Something in the ride's gears had broken, and the crew had no way to bring the gondola down. The rescue isn't in this clip. The fire brigade came with a turntable ladder and took riders off a few at a time, and they had everyone down within an hour. Nobody was reported hurt.

It wasn't the fair's first scare. A year earlier, a metal part came loose from a ride there and hurt a woman, and afterward the organizers started their own surprise checks.

The operator, Ruth Buser, whose family owned the ride, said they'd had one piece of luck. If the gondola had jammed upside down, her crew would have had to turn the whole ride by hand before anyone could reach the riders.

### CASE 10 — GREEN PLAZA

The clock in the top corner of this security camera reads just before a quarter past midnight on Friday, May 26, 2023, so really it's still late Thursday night. The camera hangs over a walkway in Green Plaza, one of the busiest malls in Alexandria, Egypt. Its indoor play area is still full of families.

The ride in the middle is a spinning pendulum. A ring of outward-facing seats spins on the end of an arm, and that arm swings back and forth between two yellow frames. Three riders are strapped in while people lean on the fence to watch.

For almost a minute it's just a ride doing what it does. When the clock passes a quarter past twelve, watch the top of the swing.

That's the whole seat ring, with the riders still in it.

Within seconds the walkway floods with people. Some families grabbed their children and left, while others tried to lift the wreck off the people underneath. Six went to the hospital, the three riders and three people who'd just been standing nearby, some with broken bones. Nobody died. Half the people hurt never even got on the ride. I didn't know a ride could sit in a mall walkway with nobody's license covering it, but that's where this one was.

Whose ride was it, the mall's or the play area's? On paper, neither. East Alexandria's district chief said the play area was licensed in the mall's name, but this ride sat outside the licensed area. It belonged to someone else, who'd set it up in the walkway with the park manager's agreement.

Prosecutors went through this same footage, and the maintenance manager and the operations supervisor were held for four days on suspicion of negligence, which both of them denied. An engineering committee was set up to find out what broke, but its findings still haven't been released, and no charges have been reported.

### CASE 11 — SAILING SHIP COLUMBIA

The Sailing Ship Columbia is a 110-foot replica tall ship that's circled the Rivers of America at Disneyland on an underwater rail since 1958. On Christmas Eve in 1998, holiday crowds were packed onto the Frontierland dock, waiting to board her.

Luan Phi Dawson was in that line with his wife, Lieu Thuy Vuong. He'd escaped Vietnam and become a Microsoft engineer.

The ship came in, and a cast member looped a mooring line around a cast-iron cleat on the bow, the fitting a rope gets tied to. But the Columbia was still moving.

The line pulled tight, and something had to give. It wasn't the rope. The cleat's bolts sheared, and about nine pounds of iron tore out of the hull and into the boarding line. It struck Dawson and his wife in the head, and the cast member was hurt too.

Dawson never regained consciousness and died two days later. His wife survived, badly hurt.

In March 1999, Cal OSHA, California's workplace safety agency, found that Disney's own manual required eight hours of training before anyone worked the Columbia. The employee on the dock hadn't had it, and she'd never docked the ship before. Her first try came on Christmas Eve, in front of a packed dock, and I doubt I'd have done better.

The second finding was worse. That cleat was built to hold a ship at rest, not to stop a moving one, and its bolts were already bent.

Disney disputed the report but paid $12,500, the most the law allowed then, and the family's lawsuit reportedly settled for around $25 million. The Columbia still sails, and after that day, dock crews were barred from stopping her with a rope.

### CASE 12 — TERRA MÍTICA INFERNO

Terra Mítica sits just outside Benidorm, on Spain's coast. On July 7, 2014, an Icelandic family on vacation made a day of it there, and their 18-year-old son lined up for Inferno with his father and brother.

Inferno is a ZacSpin, where riders sit on the outside of the track in seats that flip them head over heels.

On a sharp downward turn, his restraint gave way. He was thrown from the seat and died shortly afterward in the ambulance. It was the first death at the park since it opened in 2000.

Gröna Lund in Stockholm and other parks with the same model shut theirs down for a while.

A court took almost three years to work out why the harness didn't hold, and the answer was smaller than you'd guess.

Had the nylon strap just come undone? It hadn't. Something had cut it. A plastic cover on the seat could be knocked loose by less than about 150 pounds of force. Once it shifted, sharp edges sliced the strap, and that opened a gap of about ten inches between the bar and the seat.

The court called the cover a probable design defect and didn't find a crime by anyone. In March 2017, an appeals court kept the case closed, so nobody's ever been charged. The maker has since built that piece into the seat itself.

## LEVEL 3 — DARK WATERS

Down in dark waters, the question shifts from what broke to who was supposed to be watching.

### CASE 13 — RIDE OF STEEL

On July 8, 2011, James Hackemer went to Darien Lake, in New York, with his family. He was 29 and an Iraq War veteran. In 2008, a roadside explosive device had taken both his legs, and he'd had to learn to speak again.

He picked Ride of Steel, a 208-foot coaster that hits 73 miles an hour and holds riders with only a lap bar and a seat belt. A sign at the entrance said you couldn't ride without two legs.

How did he end up in the front seat?

He came in through the exit, the accessible entrance, so nobody measured him. His 19-year-old nephew lifted him into the seat. On the platform, three attendants, all between 18 and 24, could see he didn't have legs. They checked the bars, the board showed green, and they sent the train.

On the last of three big hills, the train crested and his cap flew off. Witnesses saw him reach for it. With no legs to brace under the bar, he lifted right out of the seat. He fell from the train and died instantly. When the train came back, his seat was empty, and the bar and belt were still locked.

The ride had worked perfectly. The state cited the park for operator error, and the sheriff said Darien Lake broke its own policies but found no criminal wrongdoing.

Let's go back to that sign. All five staff on the ride had passed written safety exams and signed that they understood the rules, and those rules said two legs. Why they let him on anyway was never explained. I don't think a green light on a board should ever outrank the sign at the entrance.

His mother said it'd help a little that he'd been happy. Two weeks later, once every operator had been retrained, Ride of Steel reopened.

### CASE 14 — NEW TEXAS GIANT

The first drop on the New Texas Giant falls 147 feet at 79 degrees, which is about as close to a wall as I'd ever want to ride. The Six Flags Over Texas ride opened in 1990 as the world's tallest wooden coaster, and in 2011 it came back as a hybrid, wood underneath and steel track on top.

On the evening of July 19, 2013, Rosa Esparza, 52, from Dallas, got on with her family. Witnesses said she was worried before the train left the station, because her lap bar didn't feel right. The train climbed anyway.

About a minute in, on that first steep descent, she came out of her seat and fell. The medical examiner ruled it an accident. The ride closed that night.

In September, Six Flags said mechanical failure wasn't to blame, though it never published an internal report. The coaster reopened with redesigned lap bar pads and seat belts, which it hadn't had before.

Her family sued in October, and whose fault it was depends on which filing you read. Six Flags claimed the trains, built by Gerstlauer, were unreasonably dangerous. Gerstlauer claimed Six Flags caused the accident, because staff hadn't stopped the ride when a supervisor noticed her bar sat too high. A park employee said in a deposition that her bar didn't look secured.

Gerstlauer also said Six Flags had asked in writing for no seat belts, on the same ride that reopened with them. I'd have liked to see that argued in front of a jury.

It never got that far. In November 2014, weeks before trial, all three sides settled on undisclosed terms, and nobody was ever found at fault.

### CASE 15 — HOTEL EMBRUJADO

Francisco Javier was 22 and worked for a wholesale fruit business in Seville. In August 2005 he came to Madrid with his sister and his girlfriend, and on August 13 they spent the afternoon at Parque Warner with a cousin.

Hotel Embrujado, the Haunted Hotel, starts as a walk-through. Then you're seated in a banquet hall, one bar comes down across your whole row, and the room starts to swing around you.

He sat between his girlfriend and his cousin under the same bar. His sister was sitting elsewhere, and she said hers wouldn't let her move at all.

Here's how his family told it. When the room began to turn, he felt dizzy and stood up, and his cousin said the bar lifted without any trouble. He grabbed what looked like a ledge on the wall, but it was part of the moving machinery, and it struck him. According to his sister, the ride didn't stop until the cycle was over. He was flown by helicopter to a Madrid hospital and died there.

The park's story wasn't the same. It described a panic attack, and a guest who'd forced his way out of the restraint. The Guardia Civil, Spain's national police force, found no sign of mechanical failure at first.

Afterward, staff told his cousin the room was watched on camera. So who was watching it?

The family took it to court, and a judge in Valdemoro convicted two park employees, the maintenance manager and the woman responsible for the ride's monitors. The fines were small, 12,000 and 3,600 euros. The family said the verdict proved nobody had been watching the screens, and in March 2007 a Madrid appeals court upheld both convictions in full.

## LEVEL 4 — THE ABYSS

The abyss is where the design itself was the problem, and nobody caught it until it was too late.

### CASE 16 — PERILOUS PLUNGE

When Knott's Berry Farm opened Perilous Plunge in September 2000, it was billed as the tallest, steepest water ride in the world. Its boats dropped 115 feet at close to 78 degrees.

Almost exactly a year later, on September 21, 2001, Lori Mason-Larez rode it with her family. She was 40, a mother of five from Duarte. During the descent she came out of the boat, and she was killed.

The ride shut, and the arguing started.

The manufacturer, Intamin, pointed to the coroner's report. Its president said her waist was about eight inches bigger than the boat's 50-inch belt, and that she never should've been let on. The family's lawyer said marks on her legs showed she'd been belted in. And the only rule posted at the ride was a four-foot minimum height.

There wasn't a word about size. Would you have read that sign and thought you might not fit? I wouldn't have.

In March 2002, Cal OSHA mostly sided with the family. It ruled the restraints inadequate and said they'd contributed to her death, with her size and the position of the belt listed as factors. Knott's couldn't reopen it without changing the restraints or limiting who could ride. Her family sued the park and Intamin.

The boats came back with four-point harnesses, and the ride lasted another decade. Perilous Plunge closed in September 2012, and three new rides took its place.

### CASE 17 — MINDBENDER

Rod Chayko and David Sager were the last two to board Mindbender on June 14, 1986, so the friends got the back car. In front of them sat an engaged couple, Tony Mandrusiak and Cindy Sims. The ride had been briefly shut that evening, then reopened.

Mindbender ran inside West Edmonton Mall in Canada, the biggest shopping mall anywhere in the mid-eighties. It had opened in December 1985 as the tallest indoor coaster in the world, about 145 feet of steel with three vertical loops.

After the big drop, heading for the third loop, the back car started to fishtail. Its wheel assembly had come apart. Chayko said his lap bar just let go, and there wasn't anything else holding them in.

The train didn't have the speed for the last loop. It rolled backwards, and the wrecked car slammed into a concrete pillar. Somewhere in all that, the four riders in the back car were thrown out.

Tony and Cindy didn't survive, and neither did David. Chayko spent more than six months in the hospital and had to learn to walk again. He didn't find out his best friend was dead until three days later.

A provincial inquiry found the bolts holding the wheel assembly had worked loose and fallen out. How many bolts were holding it on? Four. I honestly expected a bigger number than that. The inquiry didn't blame the mall. It blamed design and manufacturing faults by the West German builder.

Mindbender came back in 1987 with shorter trains and seat belts, and it ran for another 35 years before it closed for good on January 30, 2023. Chayko spent decades asking for a memorial at the site, and there's never been one.

## LEVEL 5 — THE DEEPEST

And at the very bottom are the days parks would rather forget, plus one ride that kept running after it had already killed someone.

### CASE 18 — BLACK SUNDAY

On Sunday, June 9, 1991, Kings Island in Ohio had the worst night in its history. Two unrelated accidents happened about 75 minutes apart.

At around 8:30, a 22-year-old named Timothy was crossing a wooden walkway over the lake. He reached down to splash the water, a jolt went through him, and he fell in.

His friend Eddie Haithcoat jumped in after him. So did Darrell Robertson, a 20-year-old park security guard. I don't know that I'd have been that brave.

But the water was live.

A pump that kept the lake moving had surged, and it didn't have working ground-fault protection, the safety switch meant to cut the power the instant electricity leaks where it shouldn't. Eddie and Darrell were both killed. Timothy, the man they'd gone in to save, survived with burns.

And the night wasn't over. At about 9:45, across the park, Candy Taylor, 32, from Toledo, rode Flight Commander, where riders steered two-seat pods with a joystick and could roll them all the way over.

Candy was alone in her pod. At some point she slipped out of her restraint into the empty seat beside her, which had nothing holding it closed. When the pod went upside down, she fell from the pod, and she died in the hospital.

The coroner found she'd been drinking, but officials blamed a design flaw. A ride that flips shouldn't have an open seat you can slide into.

OSHA fined Kings Island $23,500 over safety violations at the lake. Flight Commander came back in 1992 with seat dividers and new restraints, and it was gone after 1995.

Three people died in one night, and fans of the park still call it Black Sunday.

### CASE 19 — BURIED ALIVE

Buried Alive was part of Ocean Park's 2017 Halloween festival in Hong Kong. You went in on your own and lay down in a coffin. Then the bottom dropped away, and you slid down into a pitch-black maze.

On Saturday, September 16, at around two in the afternoon, a 21-year-old university student, Cheung Chiu-kit, went in. He didn't come out.

Somewhere in the dark, he took a wrong turn and ended up behind the attraction, in a staff-only area where the coffin machinery moved. When the machinery stopped, a staff member went to check and found him unconscious. He'd been struck by a moving part, and he died in the hospital. It was the first death at the park's Halloween event, which had run since 2001.

Inspectors found nothing had broken, so lawmakers asked the obvious question. Why wasn't that area locked? Police sources suggested he may have missed a dimly lit warning sign.

At the inquest, a trainer testified that performers inside should've stopped him. The coroner ruled it an accident.

In a haunted house, a figure moving through the dark is part of the show. I think that's exactly why nobody saw a guest in trouble.

The government ordered it shut, and Buried Alive never reopened. His family sued the park the next year, and at the inquest, the park said it had revised its safety rules and added lighting.

### CASE 20 — FORMULE 1

Parc Saint-Paul is a small family park in the woods north of Paris. In 2005 it bought a compact coaster from a Russian maker and named it Formule 1. Three weeks after it opened, the brakes failed and eleven people were slightly hurt.

In August 2009, a 35-year-old woman riding with her husband was thrown out on a turn and died. An expert's report blamed what it called inappropriate behavior, and the park was cleared. Seat belts went onto the lap bars, and Formule 1 kept running for another eleven years.

On July 4, 2020, Élodie Duval came to the park with her family for her son's birthday. She was 32 and rode with her partner. According to the park's owner, she was lifted out of her seat over two bumps near the end. Her partner grabbed her and held on, but he couldn't keep his grip, and she died.

This time, prosecutors found the seat belts added after 2009 weren't there anymore. They'd been taken off just before the park reopened after lockdown that June. The first locking notch on every lap bar had also been ground down, which kept the largest guests off the ride. The park hadn't reported either change to inspectors, and the owner, Gilles Campion, admitted both.

The one fix that came out of the first death was the exact thing removed before the second. I'd call that the worst decision anyone made in this whole iceberg, and I don't think it's close.

In November 2025, a court in Beauvais convicted Campion. He got a three-year suspended sentence and a five-year ban from working in amusement parks, and Élodie's mother said justice had been done.

The park had already been sold by then, and on January 29, 2026, Formule 1 was torn down.
