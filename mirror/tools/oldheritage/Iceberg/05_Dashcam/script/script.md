## CASE 01 — THE EXCAVATOR ARREST

On the evening of June 14, 2022, two Vermont State Police troopers drove up a dirt driveway on Scott Road in Hardwick. It's a town of about 3,000 people in the state's Northeast Kingdom. They'd come for one man. Brandon Tallman, 24, was wanted in an assault and burglary investigation from Woodbury, the next town over.

It should've been a simple pickup, and it wasn't. When the troopers took hold of Brandon, his mother, Amy, grabbed her son and wouldn't let go. Within seconds the troopers were down on the driveway wrestling with both of them.

The dashcam in a cruiser parked behind the police SUV is pointed right at the struggle. While the troopers were tied up on the ground, Brandon's father, Wayne, a 52 year old who ran an excavating business, had climbed into the family's excavator. Keep your eye on the long orange arm rising behind the cruiser, and on that bucket once it starts to swing.

Police later said Wayne moved the bucket recklessly near the troopers and their cruisers. No shot was fired, and nobody on either side was hurt.

Brandon was arrested, and so was Wayne. Amy was cited for impeding an officer. When State Police released the video nine days later, a commander admitted they don't have a scenario at the academy where they practice this one.

Wayne was first charged with attempted murder, but that count was dropped. In September 2022 he pleaded guilty to three felonies, impeding a public official and two counts of aggravated assault with a deadly weapon. The weapon was the excavator. He got two to four years, all of it suspended except 90 days in jail.

Both troopers told the court that was too light, and I can't say I blame them. Ninety days for swinging a steel bucket over two officers wrestling on the ground is a hard number to swallow. Judge Justin Jiron took the deal anyway, with one condition. Wayne can only operate an excavator under a supervisor his probation officer approves, and that supervisor can't be his son.

And that's only where this starts. Still to come, a small plane drops onto a Florida road right in front of a patrol car, and an escaped prisoner talks his way past a police officer at a railroad crossing.

## CASE 02 — THE KHAYELITSHA AMBUSH

Khayelitsha is one of the largest townships in South Africa, out on the Cape Flats about 19 miles from central Cape Town. Just after one in the afternoon on Friday, April 9, 2021, a car was driving down one of its roads, past corrugated iron shacks and people walking on the verges, near the area known as Site B.

The camera's stamp reads 36 kilometers an hour, about 22 miles an hour. A green and white bus is coming the other way, and the driver slows almost to a stop to let it squeeze past. It's lunchtime on a busy street, and that's what makes this so hard to believe. Look at the edge of the road as the car halts beside the bus.

A local news site said three armed men ran at the car from both sides, and that the driver found himself staring down the barrel of a gun. He didn't wait to find out what came next. It's over in about five seconds.

The clip surfaced two weeks later on the channel of a South African anti-hijacking trainer, under the title "Alert driver escapes carjacking."

## CASE 03 — THE CALAIS BRANCH ROADBLOCK

This camera sits high in the cab of a truck, a little after ten at night on September 6, 2016. It's on the raised road that carries freight into Calais, in northern France, the last stretch before the ferries to Britain. For a few seconds there's nothing on screen but an empty curve and a concrete barrier.

That year, thousands of people were living in a camp outside town known as the Jungle, and many were trying to reach Britain hidden in the back of trucks. Branches dragged into the lanes were a known way to make a driver stop long enough to climb aboard. The day before this was filmed, truckers had joined farmers in blocking the highway to demand the camp be shut.

The thing to watch is the top of that concrete barrier, and the dark tangle lying across both lanes.

Online, this clip usually gets told with another story attached, about rocks and concrete through a windshield and a driver who was badly cut. That's a different attack on a different truck, months earlier. In this one, nobody's seen throwing anything at the cab, and the glass doesn't break.

So who was behind the wheel, and did anyone get into the trailer? Nobody's ever reported either. Seven weeks later the Jungle was cleared.

## CASE 04 — THE ROADBLOCK IN THE DARK

In 2012, Russians bought about five times as many dashcams as the year before, mostly to have proof of what happened to them on the road. This one was rolling down a wet two lane road outside St Petersburg on the night of September 8, close to half past ten.

Up ahead, a car's stopped beside a striped roadside pole, with its red lights glowing. Keep your eye on the road just past that pole, and the leafy branch and row of bottles lying across it.

So was it a trap? Even the man filming couldn't say. He uploaded the clip the next day under a title meaning ambush, with a one line description. "Hold-up, robbery or hooliganism." Everything else on his channel is ordinary St Petersburg traffic. I trust his shrug more than anything in the comments.

People have called it a failed ambush ever since, but that's a theory. Nobody's ever been identified, and who put that branch there, or why, is still unknown.

## CASE 05 — FIGURE IN THE ROAD AT BUKIT TUNKU

Aswan Yap was driving through Bukit Tunku with a friend one Friday night in May 2017, somewhere between Lake Gardens and Kenny Hills. It's a quiet hill district in the middle of Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, where the road winds through patches of jungle. Local stories call it one of the city's most haunted streets, though none of them has ever been backed by a record.

They round a bend marked with yellow chevrons, and Yap stops the car. The shape is tiny at this distance, so look at the center line far up the road, where the headlights fade out.

Yap later said he was convinced it was a ghost, and that his car's log showed a fault at the moment he saw the figure. Others thought it'd been someone waiting for a car to stop so they could rob the driver. No police report was ever made public, and some people online have doubted the video is real at all.

That fault isn't as spooky as it sounds, though. The warning on his dashboard was the traction control, and his friend offered a plain reason on the spot, wondering out loud if they'd reversed too fast. I like that the clip hands us the ordinary answer before anyone online can dress it up.

## CASE 06 — JEEP FLIES INTO AN OFFICER ON HIS PHONE

Most police dashcams look out at the road, but one of the two in this Tulsa cruiser points straight back at the officer driving it. It's about 7:30 on a Thursday evening, August 15, 2019, and he's heading south on Riverside Drive near East 66th Place, a wide road with a grassy median down the middle. The department's release shows both cameras side by side.

There's a phone in his hands, down near his lap. Watch his eyes on the inside camera, and count how often they drop to that screen over the next half minute.

The Jeep Grand Cherokee came from the northbound lanes, crossed the median and hit him square on the driver's side. The officer went to the hospital with a sore jaw and elbow, most likely from the airbag. The Jeep's driver wasn't hurt.

Tulsa Police released both videos about ten weeks later, and I'll give them some credit for that, since the inside camera doesn't exactly flatter their own man. People noticed the phone right away. A neighbor told local reporters it possibly could have been prevented had he not been texting.

So was he disciplined? His name was never released, and the department never said. Officially, police put the blame on the Jeep driver, and Officer Jeanne Pierce, speaking for the department, said there was nothing the officer could have done.

## CASE 07 — PLANE FALLS ONTO KEENE ROAD

Sunday, November 19, 2017, was a clear morning in Clearwater, Florida. Two Pinellas County deputies were driving up North Keene Road, a four lane street lined with houses, on their way to an unrelated call.

Above them, a small four seat plane was heading home. Pilot Marc Benedict and his passenger had flown out to Zephyrhills that morning and topped up the tanks there. Now they'd started the short hop back to Clearwater Air Park, about a mile from where the deputies were.

Descending through 1,200 feet, Benedict switched the fuel selector from the left tank to both. About ten seconds later the fuel flow on his gauge fell from 8 gallons an hour to nothing, and the engine quit. He switched back to the left tank. It wouldn't restart. He couldn't reach the runway from that height, and below him he could see a deputy's car and what looked like an opening on Keene Road. So that's where he went.

The plane comes in at the very top of the frame, right above the traffic.

The NTSB, the federal agency that investigates plane crashes, said the video shows a controlled, level descent. Benedict told deputies his left wing caught a tree in the median, and the plane ended up in a stand of trees beside the road. He and his passenger climbed out without a scratch, and they didn't want treatment.

So why did the engine quit? Benedict hadn't checked his fuel for contamination before leaving Zephyrhills, and investigators went looking. They test ran the engine that December and bench tested a key part of the fuel system the following May. They found some debris, but it likely wasn't enough to cause a total loss of power. The NTSB's final word was that the engine lost all power for reasons that could not be determined.

## CASE 08 — ESCAPED KILLER TALKS HIS WAY PAST A COP

Richard Lee McNair was serving two life sentences when he got out of the federal prison at Pollock, Louisiana, on the morning of April 5, 2006. In 1987 he'd killed a man named Jerry Thies during a burglary at a grain elevator in North Dakota, and shot a second man there. That morning he'd hidden inside a pallet of mailbags that was moved to a warehouse outside the fence, and when the staff went to lunch, he cut his way out.

It wasn't his first escape. He'd slipped out of handcuffs at a police station in 1988, and in 1992 he got out of a North Dakota prison and stayed free for about nine months.

Just after four that afternoon, Ball police officer Carl Bordelon spotted a man in a white vest and shorts running along the railroad tracks, and waved him over. His dashcam caught the next ten minutes. Listen to how easily the man answers.

So how did he walk away? The description Bordelon radioed for didn't fit. The prison's picture showed a full beard, and the man on the tracks had a goatee and a buzz cut. Bordelon even joked that he was matching up to him. A few minutes after calling himself Robert Jones, the man gave his name as Jimmy Jones, and the slip went right past.

After about ten minutes Bordelon let him go, with advice to carry some ID next time he went jogging.

Bordelon later watched his own tape with the prison guards and realized who he'd let walk away. He kept his job, rose to assistant chief, and died in 2015 at 51.

McNair stayed free for 18 months. Within days he was on the US Marshals' 15 Most Wanted list. That same month, Canadian police questioned him over a stolen car in Penticton, British Columbia, and he ran across a field and got away. On October 25, 2007, a rookie officer with the Royal Canadian Mounted Police chased his stolen van through Campbellton, New Brunswick, then caught him on foot. "In typical McNair fashion, he jumped out of the van and took off," a North Dakota sheriff said afterward. Today he's reportedly held in a high security federal prison in Kentucky.

## CASE 09 — THE N4 ESCORT AMBUSH

The N4 runs right through Pretoria, South Africa, and on the morning of April 22, 2021, an armored Toyota Land Cruiser was out on it running an escort job. It was guarding a load of mobile phones reported to be worth millions of rand. Behind the wheel was Leo Prinsloo, a former member of the South African Police's Special Task Force who'd become a firearms instructor and started his own shooting academy in Centurion. Beside him sat Lloyd Mtombeni, who was reportedly on his fourth day in the job.

Crews that hit cash and cargo vans in South Africa often come with several cars and rifles. Three cameras were rolling in this one. The top panel shows the cab, the middle one the road ahead, and the bottom one is a third camera looking out through the glass. Watch for a dark car closing in, and don't lose track of Prinsloo's hands.

The attackers came in at least two vehicles, an Audi A5 Sportback and a white pickup, and reports said the Land Cruiser was hit more than 30 times. The cab's glass was reinforced, and it held. Police said Prinsloo evaded them for a while, then stopped and waited for them, and that they fled without taking anything. They also said no arrests had been made, and there hasn't been one reported since.

Prinsloo's voice is what I'd play back for anyone. He sounds like he's reading out directions.

Mtombeni said later that it'd been his first time getting shot at sitting in a vehicle. Prinsloo said there wasn't any way he was letting them take it. Neither man was hurt.

About ten days later the footage leaked and went around the world. More angles came out within the week. The attention also brought death threats, and his employer put Prinsloo and his family under protection.

## CASE 10 — THE PAPER MILL BLAST

A pulp digester is a steel pressure cooker the size of a building, where wood chips get cooked in caustic chemicals until they break down into the fiber that becomes paper. The one called Digester A, at the Androscoggin Mill in Jay, Maine, stood about 166 feet tall and held some 260,000 gallons. It was built in 1964, and the mill had changed hands just 66 days before this footage was shot.

Around noon on Wednesday, April 15, 2020, trucker Rick Pratt was sitting in a line of log trucks outside the mill, waiting to unload. His dashcam was pointed at the load in front of him. Look just to the left of those logs, over the trees, where the mill stands.

Pratt's reaction isn't one I can repeat. Within seconds, wet brown debris was raining down on his truck, wood fiber and cooking liquor that'd been inside the vessel a moment before.

The digester had split open while running at about 160 pounds per square inch and over 300 degrees, and it destroyed the second digester next to it. About 170 employees and contractors were on site, many of them at lunch. Nobody was seriously hurt, and only a few were treated for breathing problems. With a blast that size and that many people around, I'd put that down to plain luck.

Federal safety investigators found the vessel had failed along a weld seam, where cracks from the caustic liquor had eaten through about 70 percent of the steel wall. The last inspection, in September 2019, hadn't caught them. They blamed inspection and weld repair practices that weren't doing their job. OSHA, the workplace safety agency, opened its own inspection but didn't issue a fine, because no OSHA standard applied.

The damage came to about $350 million. The owner, Pixelle, sued a Florida contractor, saying welds it had repaired in 2018 and 2019 had failed, and there's been no ruling reported. The company never rebuilt the pulp side. In September 2022 it announced the mill would close, and by March 9, 2023, the machines in Jay had stopped for good. When the digester blew, the mill employed 515 people.

## CASE 11 — THE BENTLEY AND THE BAT

A van is rolling down a wet country lane somewhere in Britain on a summer evening when a bright green Bentley comes up fast behind it and overtakes.

Then the Bentley brakes hard, for no reason anyone could see. That's a brake check, slamming on in front of someone to make them brake or hit you. It's crawling along so slowly that the van finally overtakes it.

That seems to be what sets the Bentley driver off. See where the green car stops when it swings back around in front.

The clip went up on Reddit and spread from there. The earliest copy dates from March 2022, and the full summer leaves suggest it was filmed a year or more before that. No arrest or charge was ever reported, and the man's never been publicly identified.

He put that bat back in the trunk as calmly as if he'd only gone to fetch an umbrella, then took a photo of the van driver, like he was the one collecting evidence. I'd love to know who he planned to show it to.

## CASE 12 — THE FALLING FOREST

This dashcam was running on a narrow forest road in steady rain, with tall trunks crowding both edges. For about 40 seconds it records nothing but wet asphalt. Look at the trees just ahead of the car.

Trees don't usually come down in a line like that. There's already debris on the road at the start of the clip, which suggests a storm had passed through, but what actually brought them down was never established. Guesses at the time ran from high winds to a landslide.

The clip spread online in February 2017, labeled Slovakia, and some posts put it in the Low Tatras, a national park of mountain forest in central Slovakia. That's never been confirmed. The camera's clock shows a winter date from years earlier, but the trees are in full leaf, so it almost certainly wasn't set.

You can hear voices inside the car afterward. No report has ever said who they were, or how they came out of it.

## CASE 13 — THE CHALLENGER IN KELLER

A YouTuber who goes by Bear With Hat was leaving a friend's house in Keller, Texas, one night in June 2016 when he noticed a white car close behind him. He thought it was a Dodge Challenger.

So he tested it. He signaled for the Interstate 35W on-ramp and cut onto the service road at the last second, but at the next overpass the white car was already waiting at the light. He made a U-turn and pulled over, and it pulled over right behind him.

When he stopped in a turning lane outside a hospital, the white car stopped right in front of him. He didn't have a dashcam, so he held up his phone, hoping to catch a plate for a 911 call. Watch the passenger side of the white car.

He said later that he swung out so close he'd have hit the passenger, the man standing beside the car, if he hadn't jumped clear. The phone fell to the floor, and that's why the picture goes dark.

He got away unhurt. His video's title asks whether it was an attempted carjacking. Close to half a million people have watched it, and nobody's ever answered that. So why did they give up? With his phone rolling around the floor, he still had the sense to head for a grocery store where he knew a police officer often parked. The moment the white car saw where he was going, it peeled away.

## CASE 14 — THE FIREWORK AT THE RED LIGHT

Nashville's Fourth of July fireworks show had just ended in 2021 when Colin Kirby started home through Midtown in his convertible, with the top down and a friend in the passenger seat. Two more friends followed behind in a pickup truck with a dashcam running.

At about 10:15 p.m., the light turned red at 17th Avenue North and Church Street, and Kirby stopped. From the pickup it's nothing at all, just two cars waiting at a light. Pay attention to the white sedan that rolls up on his left.

Police said a passenger in the back rolled the window down and threw a lit firework into the open car. It hit Kirby in the back of the head and went off somewhere between six and ten inches from it. He burned two fingers grabbing at it.

Kirby pulled over and called 911. Apart from those fingers, he had some slight hearing loss that didn't last, and his passenger wasn't hurt. He said afterward it could've just as easily landed in his lap. The blast burned a hole through the roof and scorched the trim and a door, about $3,000 in damage.

His mother, Kathy, posted the clip the next night, and within a day it was national news. Metro Nashville Police said they wanted three people believed to be in a white Infiniti G37, for aggravated assault with a deadly weapon. They called it random, and the ATF looked into it as well, but no arrest has ever been announced.

Kirby said he'd forgiven whoever did it, but he still wanted them held accountable. I'm not sure I could forgive anybody that fast with two burned fingers.

## CASE 15 — THE MUDSLIDE ON OLIVE MILL ROAD

In the early hours of January 9, 2018, two California Highway Patrol officers were driving north up Olive Mill Road in Montecito, California, on their way to a reported structure fire. A month earlier, the Thomas Fire had burned the hills above the town down to bare soil. Now a storm was sitting over those hills, and at around 3:30 that morning, about half an inch of rain fell in roughly five minutes.

The officers were Will Clotworthy and Mike Fabilla. There's nothing on their dashcam but a wet road and a yellow warning sign, though Fabilla didn't like the look of the road ahead. Look past that sign, into the dark stretch beyond it.

What hit them is called a debris flow. It's mud carrying boulders and whole trees down off burned slopes. It lifted the cruiser off the road, and for about 15 seconds the two of them were floating down the street inside it. Clotworthy grabbed the radio, but at first the signal was too weak to get through. Fabilla said he was trying to tell them they were in a mudslide, floating down Olive Mill Road.

I can't picture keeping it together enough to work a radio in a car that's floating, and Clotworthy kept at it.

They didn't head for safety, either. With lights and siren on, they drove down toward the 101 freeway and radioed a warning that a very active mudslide was coming down Olive Mill. One of them got out and shouted at people to get inside. Both men stayed on duty for nearly 24 hours.

By daylight, the debris flows had torn through Montecito. Nobody died in this shot, but 23 people died in them that morning, and one of them has never been found. The 101 stayed shut under mud until January 21.

A year later, Fabilla described the moment in the plainest words possible. He'd tried telling his partner to slow down, slow down, and all of a sudden it just hit them.

## CASE 16 — THIRTEEN SECONDS AT THE CROSSING

Harold Duncan was a police officer in Brook Park, Ohio, about 10 miles southwest of Cleveland, and on the gray, rainy afternoon of March 10, 2016, he pulled up to a railroad crossing near Route 237 with his dash camera rolling.

The bells were ringing and the gates were down. On the far side, a white AT&T work van was sitting right across the tracks.

Its driver, Richard Boga, had gone around a truck at the crossing while the lights were flashing. His front wheel dropped into the rail, and the van wouldn't budge. He'd gotten out by now, and reports said he looked calm, gathering up his things. Duncan wasn't calm at all, and he ordered Boga away. Follow the officer as he runs for that van, with the clock ticking in the corner of the frame.

So how long did he have? By the camera's clock, about 13 seconds passed between Duncan reaching that van and the Norfolk Southern train hitting it. News reports rounded that up to about 20 seconds, but the camera's count is the one I'd trust.

Brook Park Police posted the video on Facebook five days later, and it spread from there. Nobody was hurt, and the van was empty when the train hit it, though it was wrecked. Boga, the man Duncan had just walked off the tracks, got a ticket for failing to heed the crossing lights.

Thirteen seconds is nothing. You'd barely get your seatbelt off in that time.

## CASE 17 — THE BRIDGE TO MACTAN

Two bridges link Mandaue City to Mactan Island in the Philippines, the island where Cebu's airport sits. Just before ten on a Tuesday night, a car is crossing one of them, with lamp posts sliding past against a black sky and motorbikes riding along the railing on the right.

The date is September 30, 2025. Most likely it's the older of the two, a two-lane truss bridge that opened in 1973. Watch the lamp posts first, then the riders along that rail.

The car wasn't swerving. The deck itself was rolling under the traffic. They were feeling a magnitude 6.9 earthquake, first reported as a 6.7, centered about 60 miles to the north, near Bogo City, and the shaking lasted about 30 seconds.

So was that bridge about to give way? Officials shut it that night for a safety check, and it reopened a few hours later. Nobody on it was reported hurt.

Officials reported no structural damage on the newer bridge beside it, and Mandaue City later set up a joint inspection of the old one. That's half a mile of steel more than fifty years old, and I'd want that inspection too.

Northern Cebu wasn't so lucky. Houses and centuries-old churches came down across the north. In San Remigio alone, at least 100 sinkholes opened, and more than 13,000 aftershocks followed over the next three weeks.

The whole province was placed under a state of calamity. The death toll went up and down for weeks while officials checked names, and it finally settled at 79 people, with more than 550 injured.

## CASE 18 — THE RAMP OUTSIDE SANTIAGO

In Chile there's a word for boxing a driver in on the road to rob them. They call it an encerrona.

This dashcam's stamp reads February 26, 2022, just after half past midnight, on a motorway near Santiago. One upload places it near Colina, though the exact road hasn't been confirmed. A post shared with the clip said these robberies had gotten so common that drivers there leave a gap on exit ramps after dark.

The driver takes an exit ramp curving down toward an underpass, and the car ahead starts braking harder than it needs to. He hangs back. Look at that car's doors when it reaches the bottom of the ramp.

He didn't wait to find out what the men wanted. That trap only works if the driver hesitates, and he didn't give them half a second.

The month before, robberies of vehicles involving violence or threats had jumped 52 percent in Chile. No arrest was reported for this one. Earlier that month, after a similar case, police advised skipping needless late night trips and watching for cars acting oddly ahead. That gap is what got him out, and it's a habit I'd pick up on my first night driving there.

## CASE 19 — SEVEN TREES IN LINCOLN COUNTY

Ronald Wiley had his pickup on a soaked, wooded road in Lincoln County, West Virginia, just after five on a Friday afternoon, May 12, 2017. It'd been raining all day.

Eleven minutes before the time on his dashcam, the National Weather Service had put the county under a flood warning. Up to an inch and a half of rain had already fallen, and a flash flood watch had covered the area since the day before. There's no sound on the clip, so all you get is the picture. The trees to watch are on the bank as he comes around the bend.

The bank had given way. Wiley told local news the trees just started falling like dominoes, and with the road that wet, he slid right into them. One trunk came down across his windshield and another across the roof of the cab. When it was over, he counted seven trees, all tangled together around his truck.

So how did he get out? Above him, the cable and telephone lines strung over the road were still holding, and he didn't want to be under there if they let go. He put it in reverse and backed out from under the pile.

Seven trees, and the worst he'd come away with was a broken wiper and a busted corner of the grille. He wasn't hurt at all. "God was definitely watching over me," he told local news.

## CASE 20 — THE STOLEN HUMVEE

Friday, July 3, 2015, was the start of the Fourth of July weekend at Fort Gordon, the big Army post outside Augusta, Georgia, and military vehicles were out on display on Barton Field. One of them, a six-ton tan Humvee, drove off and didn't come back. Police say the man behind the wheel was Michael Warning, a 47-year-old military veteran.

The Humvee made it across the line into South Carolina. On Interstate 20, about 15 miles in near Aiken, a deputy spotted a military truck where it didn't belong, and the lights came on.

It didn't stop. Aiken County deputies chased it for about 20 miles, and according to police, it nearly hit a patrol car and at one point ran up onto a sidewalk. It also hit a guardrail and later went through a fence. Out on a long four-lane highway, with cruisers stacking up behind it, it drifted onto the shoulder and wobbled back. Stay with the Humvee as it starts to angle off toward the grass.

Six deputies made the arrest, and Warning was taken to the Aiken County Detention Center. The FBI and military police looked into it as well. Deputies said he'd be charged with attempted murder, along with possessing a stolen vehicle and failing to stop for blue lights and a siren. No plea or verdict has been reported.

I wouldn't have wanted to be in the patrol car it nearly hit. A Humvee that had been parked on Barton Field for the holiday crowds finished the night nose down in a ditch in another state.
