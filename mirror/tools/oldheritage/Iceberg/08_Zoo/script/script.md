# The Most Disturbing Zoo Incidents Ever Caught on Camera

## INTRO

These are some of the most disturbing zoo incidents ever caught on camera. They range from close calls everyone walked away from to the ones that left people badly hurt. Viewer discretion is highly advised.

## CASE 01 — THE HAT IN THE TIGER YARD

A woman's hat is lying on the wrong side of a fence at the Toronto Zoo. It's Saturday, April 16, 2016, just after 3 in the afternoon, and that fence belongs to the Sumatran tiger yard.

The tiger down there is Hari, a seven-year-old Sumatran. His exhibit has two barriers. The first is the rail visitors lean on to watch him. Behind that there's a narrow gap, and then a taller fence, which is the one that actually holds the tiger. The hat's in the gap. The woman, in a red top, decides she's getting it back, so she climbs over the rail and drops down.

Across the yard, a visitor named Jared Sales is filming through the mesh. When his video starts, she's already down there, a small red shape against the far fence. Hari has already noticed her.

From the moment he hit the wire under her, she was back over the rail in about ten seconds. He paced right below her the whole way up. She still left with the hat.

The people on the boardwalk weren't impressed. Voices on the video tell her she's a bad example for everyone else's kids, and one wonders out loud if she really risked all that for a five-dollar hat. I think that's the part the crowd couldn't get over. She didn't go back for a phone or a wallet.

Nobody from the zoo saw any of it. Spokeswoman Jennifer Tracey said the attendant watching the tigers was probably at another pen, and staff didn't find out until hours later, once the video was online. Within about two days, it had passed a million views and made the news on three continents.

The zoo insists she was never within reach of the tiger. Tracey said that "at no time would she have come into contact with the tiger," because the inner fence held. What worried her was the drop. She said the woman "easily could have broken an ankle" jumping down there, and she called the stunt "very irresponsible." Hari was only showing instinctive behavior, she said. Her advice for anyone who drops something was to tell staff, who have the equipment to get it back.

The timing made it look even worse. Just the day before, a keeper had died in a tiger attack at the Palm Beach Zoo in Florida.

The Toronto Zoo opened an investigation and asked witnesses to come forward. Its bylaws ban visitors from climbing any fence or enclosure, and if she'd been identified, she could have faced a fine of up to $2,000 and a long ban from the zoo. Nobody ever put a name to the woman in the red top, so as far as anyone knows, that hat cost her nothing at all.

## CASE 02 — SHAMBA

Mike Hodge had known his lion since the day it was born. Hodge was a British expat, and he and his wife Chrissy had built a small predator park just outside Thabazimbi, in the bush country of South Africa's Limpopo province. It's called the Marakele Predator Center.

The lion was Shamba. The family bottle-fed him as a cub, and by 2018 he was ten years old, a full-grown male and the star of the place. Visitors rode through the lion camp in a caged truck the park called the lion mobile. For a full-price ticket of 70 rand, the website promised a lion would jump up and look you in the eyes. Shamba was fed from that truck every day, and that's going to matter.

There was a routine at the end of each tour. Hodge would climb out to open the exit gate, and a staff member outside the camp would distract Shamba and lead him away from it.

His family says it was about 11:20 on Saturday morning, April 28, 2018. Hodge was showing a group around, and a tourist in the truck was filming on a phone. In the footage, Shamba walks along the fence past a small hut by the gate, with mountains behind him. Then he's out in the long grass. A gray-haired man in a gray shirt starts walking across the camp toward him. That's Mike Hodge.

That's as far as I'm going to show you. What comes next is on the record, but it isn't something to put on screen.

Shamba turned and charged, and he caught Hodge near the gate while the people in the truck screamed for help. Then there was a loud bang, and the lion let go and ran.

This is where the accounts split. The family's statement said a staff member had moved Shamba away as usual, but the lion came back and chased Hodge toward the vehicle. The video doesn't quite match that. In the video, Hodge is walking out toward the lion. There's also a reported version from a friend, who said Hodge and a ranger had gone in because a smell was upsetting one of the lions. "He is no fool around lions," the friend said, "but clearly something went wrong."

Shamba was shot dead during the rescue, and even that's got two versions. One says a staff member outside the camp fired to save Hodge's life. The other, reported at the time, says a tourist in the truck fired the shot. I doubt we'll ever get a clean answer on that.

Hodge survived with a broken jaw and wounds to his neck. His family said he was 72. He was airlifted to Milpark Hospital in Johannesburg, and within days he was walking again with a physiotherapist's help. Family spokesperson Bernadette Maguire said, "He is very upset about the loss of Shamba." The family called it a double tragedy.

So why would a lion turn on the man who'd raised him? Dr. Kelly Marnewick of the Endangered Wildlife Trust had a plain answer. Captive lions lose their fear of people, she said, and Shamba had probably come to link trucks and people with food. Her group counted about 8,000 lions in captivity in South Africa. Martie Rossouw of the NSPCA, the country's animal welfare body, said the incident "highlights the very reason why wild animals should not be kept in captivity."

By the middle of May 2018, just a few weeks later, the park was open for business again.

## CASE 03 — HAND THROUGH THE WIRE

A phone is pointed at the tiger yard of the Cohanzick Zoo in Bridgeton, New Jersey. It's a Sunday afternoon, August 18, 2024, and a woman in a dark top and white shorts is standing where she isn't allowed to be.

The zoo's tigers are two Bengal brothers, and this one is Mahesha. He arrived as a 20-pound cub in 2017 and now weighs close to 500 pounds. In front of the exhibit is a wooden fence with a sign saying climbing any zoo fence breaks a city ordinance. Past that is a strip of ground, and then the real barrier, a tall wire fence. She's climbed the wooden one. Mahesha has seen her.

Her hand was through the wire for maybe two seconds. Police said she pulled it out the moment the tiger reacted, and keeper Avery Menear put it simply: "As soon as she turned around, on the video you can see his paw go up." The police chief said the tiger snapped at her. Nobody was hurt.

I can't get past what she did after he stood up on that fence, taller than her. She stepped back, but she didn't leave. She drifted along the wire while he paced beside her. The zoo says each of those tigers has teeth over four inches long.

Witnesses sent the clip to the city, and the city passed it to police. Two days later, the Bridgeton Police Department posted it on its own Facebook page. Police said she'd "climbed over the wooden fence and enticed the tiger at the interior fence almost getting injured," and asked the public who she was. You don't see many departments put the raw video out themselves. About 24 hours later the post was deleted, but by then the clip was everywhere.

Did it work? It did, and fast. Police Chief Michael Gaimari said tips came in through the department's online tip line and led them to her. He also said that in his 36 years with the department, he'd never seen anyone get that close to the animals. John Medica, the city's recreation director, said he was "horrified."

Police also said another video showed the same woman in a restricted area at the bear exhibit that day.

On August 26, police announced the charges against a 24-year-old woman from Millville. They were one count of defiant trespassing and two city ordinance citations for climbing fences inside the zoo.

That's where the paper trail stops, and I want to be careful here. She was charged, not convicted, and no outcome from the case has ever been reported.

## CASE 04 — THE MAN ON THE LION ROCKS

Nehru Zoological Park in Hyderabad opened in 1963 on about 380 acres, and it's one of the biggest zoos in India. Its African lions live below a wall of huge red boulders, with a moat between them and the visitors. Those rocks are part of the barrier. Nobody is supposed to be up on them.

On November 23, 2021, in the middle of the afternoon, visitors spotted a man in a checked shirt up there anyway. Zoo officials said he had gone around to the back of the enclosure, jumped the moat wall and walked out along the tops of the boulders. Somebody on the visitor side started filming. He's crouched right at the edge now, and there's a lion at the foot of the rocks, right below him.

He stays put the whole time that lion paces under him.

According to reports at the time, at one point it charged at the boulder he was sitting on and tried to jump. By then, visitors had alerted the staff, and two men in khaki were on their way up the rocks.

The two of them walked him back the way he came. The zoo said he had been "rescued and caught" and handed him to the Bahadurpura police, then lodged a complaint. Its press note only said a man had been walking on the boulders of the lion moat, in a restricted zone.

Why was he up there? The zoo never said. The only answer on the record is his. He reportedly told police he climbed up there looking for diamonds, which he believed were hidden inside the lion enclosure. He was about 31. A police official said a first inquiry suggested he might be mentally unwell, and his family was contacted. I don't think that's a punchline. It's the only reason he ever gave.

What happened to him after that isn't clear. Local reports couldn't agree on whether police even opened a case, and no charge or court date was ever reported.

Back in 2016, a different man climbed all the way down into the lion enclosure at this same zoo, and a keeper had to pull him out. This man never made it down into the lions' yard. The staff got to him while he was still sitting on the rocks.

## CASE 05 — HOT CHEETOS IN THE MONKEY POOL

In May 2021, a short phone video from the El Paso Zoo in Texas started making the rounds on Instagram, and within two days it was on the national news. It had been filmed on May 22, at the spider monkey exhibit. The monkeys there live on an island of fake rock with a waterfall, and a shallow moat runs all the way around it. Between the public path and that water, there was a fence about three feet tall.

A woman in a white tank top stepped over it and pushed through the bushes to the water. Somebody on the path had a phone out, and by the time it was on her, she was already out by the monkeys' rocks.

According to the person filming, what she's handing over there is Flamin' Hot Cheetos. It almost looks cute, but the zoo's director, Joe Montisano, didn't see it that way. "It was stupid," he said. "She knew what she was doing was wrong." In his telling, she'd hopped a fence, climbed through some bushes and dropped into a "four-feet deep moat."

The monkeys are called Libby and Sunday. A year later the zoo gave their ages as 35 and 42, so Sunday was about fifteen years older than the 26-year-old feeding them. Two small monkeys and a handful of chips doesn't sound like much of a threat. "These are primates," Montisano said. "They are strong; they have canine teeth." A keeper, Mason Kleist, said they could do substantial damage to a person. The other risk was one you can't see. Both monkeys are on a special diet, and Kleist said spicy human food could upset their stomachs. And in the middle of a pandemic, she could've passed them COVID. Did they actually eat any? The zoo said it couldn't tell.

Several callers identified her once the video spread. She worked at a local law firm, and the firm announced that she "has been terminated" and called it "irresponsible and reckless behavior." On May 27, she was booked into the county jail on a criminal trespass charge and released on a $1,500 bond. That July, the zoo put up a new fence around the exhibit, about six feet tall, for nearly three thousand dollars. I'd say that's cheap, considering what the old one let through.

A year later, the charge was still pending, and she sat down with a local TV station. "I don't think I did anything wrong," she said. What she'd learned was to "just keep the Cheetos to myself." Her lawyer, Mark T. Davis, who by then was also her employer, said no animal was ever mistreated. No final ruling on the charge has ever been reported.

As for Libby and Sunday, Montisano's verdict was that they were "maybe a little shaken up by the incident but they are fine."

## CASE 06 — CAMEL STAMPEDE ON THE SAFARI ROAD

You're behind the wheel of your own car on a safari road in Puglia, in southern Italy, with a small camera stuck to the dashboard. This is Zoosafari Fasanolandia, and the park calls itself the second-largest wildlife park in Europe. The drive runs almost four miles through olive and carob trees, with animals roaming free on both sides. The rules are simple: windows closed, and you never get out of the car.

The date is August 4, 2010, and the clock on your dashcam reads 4:07 in the afternoon. Zebras graze by a fence on your left, with a small blue car ahead of you. Then the road bends, and suddenly it's full of camels and dromedaries crowding around that blue car.

Something is wrong inside the herd. As the man who filmed this tells it, the camels have turned on one of their own, so the first fight on this road isn't even about you. You can't go forward, and you wait for someone to come and clear the road. He wrote that the warden who should have been on guard wasn't at his post, and nobody came.

Then, in his words, from the right comes "a kind of mammoth." The herd panics, and it runs straight down the road at you.

The camera never shows a mammoth. What reaches you is a pale two-humped camel, and once it fills the glass, I can't really blame him for the word. Its chest is at your hood, and its head is up past your roof line. A light-blue van pulls up beside you, and the camel just stands there between the two cars, looking down.

He wrote afterward that on camera they look far away, but they were right up against the car. If they had climbed onto it, he said, he didn't know if he'd be around to tell the story. Nobody in your car is hurt. The park never commented, so that empty warden's post is still only his account.

And if it ever happens to you out there? The park's rules still spell it out: in an emergency, sound your horn so staff can step in.

## CASE 07 — THE NEW TIGER AT FEEDING TIME

According to the man who uploaded this clip, the tiger in it had just arrived at the old Thrissur Zoo in Kerala, in southern India. The zoo opened in 1885 and squeezed into thirteen and a half acres in the middle of the city. Behind the public paths stood a row of yellow arches, each one closed off with green iron bars, and those were the night cages.

Thrissur is where Kerala has sent tigers trapped in Wayanad district for raiding near villages, usually when they're too old or too hurt to go back to the wild. In July 2015, for example, a tiger trapped there after a man was killed was sent here. The video went up on January 10, 2018, and the uploader's note says this new tiger "became angry" when the keeper came with its meat.

A keeper in a khaki uniform walks up with an orange bucket and leans right in to one of the arches, his face almost touching the bars.

That's the whole lunge, a few seconds at most. The tiger got up on its hind legs with its paws on the iron and its head level with his. The keeper's reaction is the strange part, because there almost isn't one. He bends down to the bucket and starts pushing chunks of meat through the gap under the bars, his hand going in right where those paws just were. I don't know how many times he'd done that before, but it can't have been his first. He keeps it up for about forty seconds, then turns and walks off like it's a normal shift. Behind him, the tiger lies down among the meat.

Which tiger is it, though? The uploader never said, and nothing on the record ties it to a name. If it is one of those Wayanad tigers, I think that makes the keeper's calm even harder to believe.

Those yellow arches belong to the old zoo now. Its animals have since been moved, in stages, to a new park of 338 acres at Puthur, outside the city.

## CASE 08 — THE MANDRILL THAT SWAM THE MOAT

September 25, 2011, was a sunny Sunday at Zoo Augsburg in southern Germany, and the mandrill troop there was in turmoil. Since 2009, they'd lived on what used to be the zoo's baboon island, a grassy slope with climbing poles ringed by a concrete moat of dark water. Visitors lean on a metal rail and look across. That moat is the only thing between the troop and the path.

Mandrills are the largest monkeys in the world. A big male can weigh up to 66 pounds and carry canine teeth almost two inches long.

Three days before, the zoo had announced the death of Cita, who it called "the boss of the mandrill group." His health had "massively deteriorated," and he'd been put down. In the weeks after his death, the zoo wrote later, there were "massive" power struggles over who'd run things next. A new male took charge fast. When a male mandrill rises in rank, his face actually turns redder, so you'd more or less see a takeover happen on him. Among the females it didn't go so smoothly. The one who used to be on top was hurt so badly in the fighting that she couldn't be saved. The zoo never connected any of that to what happened on this Sunday.

That afternoon, a visitor was filming the troop on its climbing poles. Then several mandrills came down to the edge of the moat.

The crowd at the rail just watched one of them cross the whole moat, and almost nobody moved away from the water. Now it's climbing out right beside them, and it's heading for the railing.

Somebody had to pull a visitor back by the shoulder, and I'd say that's about the only fast move anyone made all afternoon. After that the mandrill just went for a walk along the visitor path, past the benches and later off under the trees, while families backed out of its way. When it sat down on the rail like any other visitor taking a break, I think that was the strangest moment of the lot. Most people just kept filming, and nobody got hurt.

And how did it get back? The video never shows that. Its last shots are of a man running along the path, with a crowd gathering behind him. The zoo's newsletters that fall cover Cita, the fighting and the female they lost, and none of them mentions a mandrill getting out.

## CASE 09 — THE CHEETAH WALK

A woman in a yellow top is leading a cheetah across a dusty yard at Moholoholo, a wildlife rescue center outside Hoedspruit in South Africa's safari country. There's a rope coiled on the ground, and visitors are standing around in a loose ring.

Animals that can't go back to the wild stay on at Moholoholo as ambassadors for the center's tours, and cheetahs suit that job, because they're slim and quiet and can be trained from cubs to walk on a lead. The center's trainers have said the first two years of that training are intense, though, because young cheetahs can be very jumpy.

This clip was posted in August 2014 by a Dutch family touring southern Africa. For about 20 seconds the cat pads along at her knee like a well behaved dog, right toward the people with cameras.

It has hold of her for about three seconds before a man in gray jeans grabs it, and it takes more than one pair of hands to pull it off her. That's what bothers me here. This is an animal that's supposed to be calm enough to walk on a rope.

Was she hurt? Nothing was ever reported, and in the clip she seems fine. Within half a minute the cheetah's sitting by a tree stump, and the woman in yellow is standing right next to it like nothing happened.

A year later, at a different facility called KwaCheetah, three people were bitten in five weeks. South Africa had fewer than 1,000 cheetahs then, and several hundred of them lived in 79 captive facilities.

The way I see it, the men nearest to her didn't hesitate, and that's a big part of why she walked away from it.

## CASE 10 — THE LION THAT BIT THROUGH THE FENCE

A man in a red shirt and a black cap walks up to a lion cage with a tour group behind him. It's just after four in the afternoon on May 20, 2022, near the end of a tour at Jamaica Zoo. That's a small private zoo near Lacovia, in St. Elizabeth, in the southwest of the island.

Behind the chain link are two male lions, and one of them is called Santa Cruz. At least 15 visitors are watching, and somebody's filming on a phone.

The man steps up to the wire and pushes his hand through.

Both lions snarl at him, and they don't stop. He slides his fingers into the corner of a lion's mouth and pulls them out. Then he goes back in. For the next half minute he gets every warning a lion can give through a fence, and he keeps his hand right there anyway.

The group laughs along. One woman said later she thought it was a joke at first, since putting on a show is the staff's job. I can see why they laughed. They'd taken him for staff, so they followed his lead. He even steps back and grins at the camera. Then, with the lions roaring, he walks back to the fence for one more go.

The lion closed its mouth on his hand and held on. He wrestled his hand free and fell to the ground, and the same woman said that's when everyone realized it was serious. He lost the tip of a finger. He walked to a vehicle and left.

The video was online within hours. The next day, a zoo representative said she didn't know about any incident, and a senior police source said the same.

So who was he? Early reports called him a zoo attendant. By Tuesday the zoo's statement used a different word. It named him and called him a contractor. His actions, it said, didn't reflect the zoo's safety rules, and he was recovering from relatively minor injuries. As for Santa Cruz, nothing was ever reported about him being harmed.

Within two weeks, Jamaica's environment agency had ordered the zoo closed from June 1, 2022, citing about a dozen broken permit conditions, including not training the staff who handle animals. The tourism regulator also confirmed the zoo had never held a license to operate as an attraction. The operator, Paul Fearon, said NEPA had given him the all clear weeks earlier, and that he didn't know he needed that license. I'd have thought that's the first thing you'd sort out before putting lions on a tour.

Jamaica's animal welfare society, the JSPCA, wasn't buying the idea of one bad afternoon. Older videos from the same cage had turned up, including one of a man provoking a lion with a plastic bottle back in 2016. Its managing director, Pamela Lawson, called it reckless endangerment. "You keep kicking a dog," she said. "After a while, it's going to turn around and bite you."

## CASE 11 — THE BEAR IN THE SNOWBANKS

On the morning of March 17, 2021, people in Nizhnevartovsk started calling the police about a bear on the street. It's an oil city in western Siberia with about 280,000 people, and the bears are supposed to stay in the forest outside it.

This one was a young female brown bear. Locals said she'd been wandering the city's industrial edge for about an hour from around 9 a.m., frightened and confused. One driver caught her walking along a roadside snowbank by a red and white building, with two stray dogs trailing behind. The car keeps rolling closer.

She passed within a few feet of that side window. That's a bear at rush hour, right on the other side of the glass.

Across another road, past a crash barrier and a blue striped warehouse, a second phone found her sitting in a snowbank and rolling around in it. A man's standing a few yards to her right, by a little white booth, talking on his phone. For about half a minute neither of them goes anywhere.

That rush toward him was only the start. He ran, and she chased him along the sidewalk past a car dealership. Drivers pulled people off the sidewalk and into their cars. When he dashed across the road, a city bus came through, and the bear ran straight into its path. Some reports say the bus hit her, and others say it braked just short. Either way, the man got away, and police said no one was hurt.

So where does a bear in a Siberian city come from? She came from a hotel, not the forest. Its owner, a local businessman, had bought her and her mother from a traveling circus in December 2018 to advertise the place, and the two of them lived in a cage on the hotel grounds. A hotel kept two bears as advertising, and now one of them was loose on a city road.

Police and Rosgvardia, Russia's national guard, ringed her with cars and closed the only road to the airport. She squeezed into a gap between a snowdrift and a heating main. The police chief, Fanis Khamatov, said they were waiting for the vets so they wouldn't have to destroy her, and could sedate her and move her somewhere safe instead. In the end, welders from a nearby plant reportedly built a corridor on the spot to steer her into a transport cage.

Then the owner reportedly told police he was fine with her being put down. A police spokeswoman said he didn't care at all about the animal's future, and that police definitely weren't going to do it. The mayor, Vasily Tikhonov, said he'd been flooded with appeals and had questions for the owner who'd put residents at risk.

That same day, police helped specialists move her into an enclosure. Six days later they announced they'd found no offense of any kind. I think that's the most disturbing part of the whole story. Both bears went back to the man who'd bought them from the circus.

## CASE 12 — THE LION THAT OPENED THE DOOR

A pride of lions is lying in the grass a few yards from a family car. The tawny ones are in front, and a few white lions are stretched out behind them.

This was early 2014, at a drive-through lion park in South Africa that's never been named. Two sisters were visiting their parents, who were serving a church mission there, and the family drove in to see the lions. Parks like this have one rule, posted at the gate: keep your windows closed and your doors locked.

One young lion gets up and walks straight at the car. It brushes past the window close enough for its eye to fill the frame, then it circles around to the back. Now it's at the rear passenger window, looking in. Watch that door.

The lion had used its teeth on the handle. Somebody grabbed the door, slammed it and hit the lock, and in the scramble one of them shouts, "You were supposed to lock the door." It hadn't been locked, and I don't think a lion needs a better invitation than that. Nobody inside got a scratch, and the lion went back to the pride.

Their brother, Joshua Sutherland, posted the clip on March 12, 2014, under the plain title "Lion opens car door." A year later somebody posted it to Reddit, and the video pulled in about 1.4 million views in under 48 hours.

Most reports called it a lioness. Kevin Richardson, South Africa's best known lion handler, called it a young male, and the maneless lion in the footage could be either. He wasn't surprised, and described a curious young lion that was used to people, and "a tempting piece of plastic." Lions don't have fingers, he said, so they explore with their teeth and claws. I don't find that very reassuring. To that lion, the handle was a toy, and the only thing between it and the family was a lock nobody had pressed.

The park never commented. Joshua's description under the video opens with a question, "Did you know lions could open car doors?" Then he says his sisters "had an experience of a lifetime."
