A woman in a yellow top is leading a cheetah across a dusty yard at Moholoholo, a wildlife rescue center outside Hoedspruit in South Africa's safari country. There's a rope coiled on the ground, and visitors are standing around in a loose ring.

Animals that can't go back to the wild stay on at Moholoholo as ambassadors for the center's tours, and cheetahs suit that job, because they're slim and quiet and can be trained from cubs to walk on a lead. The center's trainers have said the first two years of that training are intense, though, because young cheetahs can be very jumpy.

This clip was posted in August 2014 by a Dutch family touring southern Africa. For about 20 seconds the cat pads along at her knee like a well behaved dog, right toward the people with cameras.

It has hold of her for about three seconds before a man in gray jeans grabs it, and it takes more than one pair of hands to pull it off her. That's what bothers me here. This is an animal that's supposed to be calm enough to walk on a rope.

Was she hurt? Nothing was ever reported, and in the clip she seems fine. Within half a minute the cheetah's sitting by a tree stump, and the woman in yellow is standing right next to it like nothing happened.

A year later, at a different facility called KwaCheetah, three people were bitten in five weeks. South Africa had fewer than 1,000 cheetahs then, and several hundred of them lived in 79 captive facilities.
