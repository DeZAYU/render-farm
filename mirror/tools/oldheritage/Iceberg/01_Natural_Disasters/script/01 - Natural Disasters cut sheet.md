## CASE 01 · The wave comes up Ōfunato Bay · Ōfunato, Japan

[OFU] <https://youtu.be/jbvBsgoEAe0>

One of the people watching this harbour that afternoon ran a museum about tsunamis. His surname is Saitō. He was director of the Tsunami Memorial Hall in Ōfunato, a fishing town in Iwate Prefecture. If anyone on this coast understood what was coming, it was him. At 2:46 on the afternoon of Friday the 11th of March 2011, the seabed off northeastern Japan slipped in the biggest earthquake the country had ever measured. Saitō was on high ground above the town when the shaking began, and he stayed there. This is the harbour before the water arrives.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 1:35–2:00 · [visual: the harbour and the town below, before the wave] · ~25s · the build-up, keep it short, the first five minutes are waiting

Now, if you only know tsunamis from movies, forget the wall of water. The quake heaves up a huge stretch of sea floor, and the whole ocean above it rises with it. That water then has to travel to the shore, which is why there's a gap between the shaking and the flood. Japan's warning service used that gap. The first tsunami warning went out at 14:49, three minutes after the shaking started. The problem was what it said. The estimate for Iwate was three metres, because the quake's size had been read as magnitude 7.9. On an earthquake this big, the seismometers saturate, and the early number comes out low.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 5:00–5:28 · [visual: the sea rising over the harbour wall] · ~28s · water tops the wall at 5:07

At 15:14 the warning for Iwate went up to six metres, after a GPS buoy off Kamaishi picked up the sea rising. A three metre forecast is a number you can hold up against a harbour wall. The real wave here was many times that, and the harbour wall was never going to matter.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 6:07–6:37 · [visual: water pouring into the streets of the town] · ~30s

Ōfunato sits at the end of a long, narrow bay, and that shape is why the wave here was estimated at up to 23.6 metres. As the bay narrows, the same water has less and less room, so it climbs. Here it ran about three kilometres inland. Keep your eyes on the far end of the valley.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 7:22–7:52 · [visual: houses lifted off their foundations and carried up the valley] · ~30s

These houses had come through the earthquake. Japan's building codes for shaking are some of the strictest anywhere, and they mostly worked that afternoon. Nobody writes a code for a house that floats. Once one comes loose, it becomes the thing that hits the next one.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 7:52–8:22 · [visual: more houses drifting up the valley as the water keeps rising] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB, inside the 7:22–8:52 houses beat

The city's own records contain one line that should bother anyone who plans for disasters. Ōfunato had 58 designated evacuation sites, the places people had been told to run to. At least six of them flooded. This town had prepared for a tsunami. It had prepared for a smaller one.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 8:22–8:52 · [visual: the valley filling, wreckage drifting inland] · ~30s

Saitō knew this coast's history better than almost anyone. This coast was hit in 1896 and again in 1933. In 1960, a wave from an earthquake in Chile crossed the entire Pacific and came into this same bay, with no shaking felt here at all. His museum existed because of those dates.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 10:00–10:30 · [visual: the flooded town, debris moving through the streets] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB, beat estimated inside the 5:49–16:50 flooding range

Out in the deep ocean, a tsunami moves at roughly the speed of a jet airliner, and it only slows down as the water gets shallow. Slowing down is what makes it pile up. Around here, the water can start to look like it's settling.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 11:00–11:30 · [visual: wreckage drifting through the flooded town] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB

It hasn't. A tsunami arrives as a series, and the first wave is often not the biggest. That's why the rule along this coast is to reach high ground and stay there, even when it looks finished.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 12:30–13:00 · [visual: the town still under water, wreckage moving] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB

Up the coast in Aneyoshi, a hamlet in Miyako, there's a stone tablet left after the 1933 tsunami telling people not to build their homes below that point. The hamlet listened, and in 2011 the water stopped just short of the stone. A slab of rock carved in the 1930s gave better advice than the first forecast did.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 13:30–14:00 · [visual: the flooded valley, wreckage still drifting] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB, inside 5:49–16:50

The people who made it up the hills spent that night outside. It was March, and snow fell across parts of Tōhoku that evening.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 14:30–15:00 · [visual: the town under water from the high ground] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB

One building in town held. Ōfunato's theatre came through without damage and sheltered about 250 survivors.

▶ RAW · [OFU] 15:30–16:00 · [visual: the last minutes of the recording, the town still flooded] · ~30s · ⚠ SCRUB

Japan's weather agency changed its warnings because of days like this one. If it thinks a quake might be bigger than its first reading, it no longer gives a number at all. ## CASE 02 · Families on the road below Fuego · Escuintla, Guatemala

[FUE] <https://youtu.be/Q3jDjAZoe-Q>

In Ōfunato, the water at least had to cross the sea before it arrived. On the road below Fuego, the danger was already on the slope next to the traffic. Volcán de Fuego, the volcano of fire, stands above the towns west of Guatemala City, and it's one of the most active volcanoes on Earth. People on its southern slopes grow up watching it. So on the afternoon of Sunday the 3rd of June 2018, when it erupted again, the people on this road did what they'd always done. They stopped to look.

## CASE 05 · The night the ravines came through Aldaia · Valencia, Spain

## OUTRO

Nineteen disasters, and in almost every one, the people who came through it had a few floors or a hillside between them and the worst of it. The planet doesn't owe anyone a warning. What the people in charge do with the warnings they do get is a different story, and most of these nineteen turned on it.
