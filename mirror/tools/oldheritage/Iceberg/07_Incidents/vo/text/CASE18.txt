The Kapitan Bolsunovsky was a Russian freezer trawler, a factory ship that freezes its catch on board, and in May 2012 she'd been fishing the Bering Sea off Cape Navarin with 91 men aboard, in water packed with broken ice.

She wasn't cleared to be there. Ships that work in ice carry an ice class, a rating that says the hull's been strengthened for it, and at her last inspection the Bolsunovsky lost hers. Her captain knew it, and he didn't stop fishing in heavy ice.

On the morning of May 26, the ice won. Floes grinding against the hull split the welded seams on her left side, below the waterline, and the crew pulled on survival suits while a few of them kept filming.

Seawater poured into the holds, and when a watertight door between them gave way, she started to lean. The thing I notice most is how calm the sea looks. It's flat and white with ice, and a ship this size is rolling over in the middle of it.

She went down about 45 minutes after the ice broke through.

Every one of the 91 men survived. A trawler from the same company, the Dersu Uzala, wasn't far away, and it took them all aboard. So what happened to the man who'd taken her into the ice? The captain pleaded guilty, and in November 2012 a Russian court convicted him of breaking maritime safety rules. The owner's losses ran past 166 million rubles, and his fine was 140,000, roughly 4,500 dollars. That's not even a tenth of one percent of what the owner lost. The Bolsunovsky's still down there, about 660 feet deep.
