## CASE 01 — THE HIKER FROZEN ON THE CLIFF

On April 13, 2025, a woman hiking the Pacific Crest Trail in Southern California took a wrong turn near Whitewater, just north of Interstate 10. The path ran out on a slope of loose gray rock above a long drop, and when she tried to move, the ground wouldn't hold her. She couldn't go up or down, so she gripped the cliff with a heavy pack still on her back and sent one emergency text to 911 from her satellite messenger.

She held that same spot for more than an hour. The sheriff's office later wrote that she was clearly death gripping the cliff.

What you're watching now comes from the crew who came for her, on the Riverside County Sheriff's helicopter, Rescue 9. Their rescue specialist, Jason Beeman, rode the hoist cable down toward a tiny figure on a pale face of rock, with a camera on his helmet. When he landed beside her, her legs were shaking.

Asking her to let go and lift her arms into a rescue strap was too risky, because one slip could take her straight off the face. So he went with something much simpler, and told her to hold on to him.

Once she was on solid ground, she collapsed onto her side, then sat up and drank some water while the crew checked the scrapes down her legs. She'd been about 20 days into her hike, and she decided to end it there. The crew said afterward that he was completely confident he could hold on to her. I'm not sure I'd have felt that confident from her side of the hug.

That was only the first of 25, and trust me, they get a lot more disturbing from here. Before we're done, you'll watch a fishing trawler go down in the ice with 91 men on board. If raw moments like these are your thing, subscribe, because we've got plenty more of them coming.

## CASE 02 — LOST IN THE LOBSTER CAVE

A dive light cuts a white tunnel through black water, and the camera on this diver's head sees nothing else. It's a lobster night off Southern California, and he's out with his father at his honeyhole, slang for a place that always pays off.

Divers here can only take spiny lobsters by hand. A scared one backs deep into the rock and braces against the walls, so you follow it in. Reaching into a black hole underwater for dinner? I'd pass, and that's before anything goes wrong.

The yellow mesh bag keeps filling. Then he chases a big one further into a cave, and the rock closes in around him. He wins the grab, and somewhere in here, by his own account, he loses the way out.

He finds it again, and his father's light shows up in the water. Between them they took two limits, about 14 lobsters, plus a six-pound cabezon. When he posted the dive in December 2013, he wrote that he nearly drowned down there, and thankfully got out fine.

## CASE 03 — THE CARABINER THAT OPENED ITSELF

The Stawamus Chief is a granite dome that rises more than 2,000 feet over Howe Sound in Squamish, British Columbia. In July 2022, Casey Dubois was on the fourth pitch of a route called Warriors of the Wasteland. A pitch is one rope length of climbing, and this one's a thin finger crack about 100 feet long.

He's placed small spring loaded cams in the crack, each clipped to his rope with a carabiner, a metal loop with a spring gate. Kyle Smith's filming from above, and Casey's got one last reach to go.

He comes off.

Watch the replay. The tiny cam at his ankles rips out, and somehow the carabiner on the piece below comes off the rope. The best guess is it'd flipped as the rope went slack, though Kyle said they couldn't work out how. A carabiner's got one job, and that's staying shut, and I think a loose rock would scare me less.

Casey slid more than half the pitch before the rope caught him. His helmet cracked in several places, but Kyle said he was a little shaken and totally okay.

## CASE 04 — THE COULOIR OF DEATH

The Grand Couloir du Goûter is a steep gully on the most popular route up Mont Blanc, the highest peak in the Alps, and climbers call it the Couloir of Death. Almost everyone on that route crosses it twice, going up and coming down. It's only about 330 feet across, and the only protection is a steel cable strung over it, under a face that sheds rocks all day long. So how often does that face actually drop something?

In the summer of 2019, sensors in the couloir picked up more than 2,600 rockfalls in 68 days. That's about 39 a day.

In July 2020, Katrin Merisalu's team was on the way down from the summit, waiting at the edge of the gully for their turn to cross. Her phone was recording. Out in the middle, on a band of ice and snow, one climber in dark clothes was crossing alone.

Then the mountain lets go.

This time, Merisalu wrote, it all ended well. Between 1990 and 2011, accidents on this stretch of the route killed 74 people, so I doubt that's a sentence anyone gets to write very often. The climber was rescued later by helicopter, and that's about all anyone knows.

## CASE 05 — HOISTED OFF LILY ROCK

High on the white granite dome above Idyllwild, California, in October 2023, one of two climbers fell at about 7,500 feet and broke his ankle, and both of them would leave that rock on a cable. Locals call it Lily Rock, and on maps it's Tahquitz Rock.

We're only seeing what came after, through the helmet cameras of Rescue 9, the same Riverside County Sheriff's helicopter. It's hanging close to the granite while two small figures wait, clipped into a crack below. A rescue specialist rides the cable down to their ledge and clips the first climber in. For me, the worst part of a ride like this is the second they both step off.

They swing out together over the drop.

Then the cable goes back down for the second climber, in a red jacket. There was a rescue on that rock the same day this video went up, and it's very likely these two. One climber was flown to the hospital with moderate injuries, and the other turned down treatment.

## CASE 06 — HELD UNDER BELOW GAGE'S FALLS

Ted Engelhardt, a creek paddler from around Erie, had wanted to run Belson Creek for years. It's a tiny snowmelt creek in a deep gorge near Ripley, New York, and he'd once camped down there alone for four days to learn it and clear fallen trees. That was four days in a gorge for one run, and I'm not sure I would have had the patience.

On March 14, 2015, the creek was high with meltwater. Ted and his friend Frank put on at 4:30 in the afternoon, with nobody on the bank holding a rope. It was getting late, so Ted scouted Gage's Falls in a hurry.

The red bow tips over the lip.

The boat lands in the boil and stalls. The hole rolls him for close to a minute, and about halfway through he's out of the kayak. His GoPro sat about 11 inches above his mouth, so the camera is often above water when he isn't. How can you tell when he's under? He wrote that if you can't hear him moaning, he probably is.

A deep pool saved him. He balled up and sank, and the current flushed him downstream. After that, the camera catches his bare hands on the gorge wall.

He swam another 300 feet, then the pair hiked a mile and a half through knee deep snow in the dark. His empty boat circled in that hole for over 20 minutes. The accident report he filed himself logs his injury as hypothermia. His lesson afterward was never to run it with fewer than three people.

## CASE 07 — CAPSIZED IN THE WHITECAPS

Before sunrise one fall morning in 2023, an angler who posts as FishWithDaddy launched his yellow kayak on Broken Bow Lake in southeastern Oklahoma. The wind was up, and he called the dark crossing pretty sketchy.

By late morning he hadn't caught a thing. The open water back to the marina was whitecapping. Another angler on a green kayak pulled up, and they agreed to cross together. At least the water's about seventy degrees, they joked.

Out in the open water, the wind's hitting them side on. Then he looks across.

The green kayak was upside down, its owner in the water beside it. He couldn't pull a grown man onto a loaded kayak in that chop, so what could he do? He stayed, and there's the line I like most, because nothing made him say it. "Well, I gotta go with you." He called the marina and was told to call 911, and he kept checking the man wasn't cold. He paddled beside him all the way to the island, and they flipped the green kayak back over.

## CASE 08 — TOWED OUT TO SEA

Kim and Bill are a married couple who film their fishing trips, and in June 2019 they paddled two yellow kayaks through the surf at Matagorda, Texas. It was only Kim's second day ever fishing beyond the breakers, out past the waves near an offshore rig, in a north wind of 15 to 18 miles an hour.

Kim hooked something on a chunk of jack. It didn't run. It just pulled, steadily, away from shore. Bill paddled over and they held their kayaks together, so now it towed both.

After about 30 minutes Kim's arms were done, and Bill took the rod while the rig kept shrinking behind them. Whatever's on the other end has now dragged two loaded kayaks a long way from where they started, and it still hasn't shown itself.

Then a fin breaks the surface.

It's a bull shark, one of the three species most likely to bite people, and they put it at close to ten feet. Kim didn't want to be anywhere near it, and on her second day out past the breakers, I can't blame her. Bill got it beside his kayak again and again, until the knot gave out, probably at the swivel. By their count the fight ran about an hour and 15 minutes, and it had dragged them more than a mile off the rig.

## CASE 09 — THE PARATROOPER WHO MISSED THE DROP ZONE

You stand in the open side door of a C-130, 1,200 feet above the ground, with a camera on your helmet. This is a static line jump, so a cord tied to the aircraft pulls your chute open as you leave.

Then you're out. Your T-10D blooms overhead, and round canopies drift down around you. It all looks calm, but the wind up high is blowing 30 knots, and the T-10D can't be steered, so it goes wherever the wind decides to take it.

The ground starts sliding sideways under your boots, faster than it should.

The man behind that helmet camera was a US Army paratrooper who posted the clip in 2011. By his own account, he came down about 200 meters past his target, in a tank ditch, and his right leg broke badly. After 12 surgeries he chose amputation below the knee, and the Army medically retired him in 2014. Would he do it again? He says he wouldn't change a thing, even knowing he'd lose the leg. I can't imagine saying that. He has since gone back to jumping a few times a year from a World War Two era C-47.

## CASE 10 — THE MAN PAST THE RAILING AT MATHER POINT

On June 3, 2017, at around three in the afternoon, Mather Point was packed. It's the busiest viewpoint on the Grand Canyon's South Rim, and a visitor named Cyndi was there for her birthday weekend, filming over the railing.

Then the crowd started calling out. A barefoot man in his twenties had climbed over the guard rail and sat down on a rock ledge a few feet below it, with the canyon dropping away behind him. By Cyndi's account, he'd been drinking.

I keep coming back to how Cyndi put it afterward. Past that rail, she said, you're on your own responsibility. Everyone keeps telling him to come back up, and he keeps saying he will, but he doesn't move.

They ask him one more time.

He didn't come back up. Seconds later he lost his grip and went over. Somebody shouted not to look, and someone called 911. A helicopter was over the rim in about five minutes, Cyndi wrote, but reaching him took hours.

This is the helicopter that came for him.

Three responders were lowered down the cliff to stabilize him. Then he was short-hauled out, hanging on a rope under the helicopter, and taken to Flagstaff Medical Center. So did he make it? For weeks, Cyndi had no idea. Then word reached her through his family that he was alive, with some brain injury. The park put his total fall at about 100 feet, and he survived it.

## CASE 11 — THE BINDING THAT WOULDN'T LET GO

Bryce Bigelow's helmet camera spends almost three minutes in fresh powder at Brighton Resort in Utah, weaving through heavy pines behind a friend. It's January 15, 2023, and I could watch that run on loop.

Then he lines up on a drop under the Snake lift and jokes that he's going to jump off it and get wrecked. The lip is way bigger than it looked. In the air he panicked and tucked, then started to turn.

## CASE 12 — THE HUMAN CHAIN AT SAIRAN

In Almaty, Kazakhstan, the Ulken Almatinka river reaches Lake Sairan through a steep channel of smooth concrete. In late May 2016, fast brown floodwater was tearing along the foot of that wall, and a small black dog was stuck in the corner where the wall meets the water.

A young man climbed down and reached it. But he couldn't climb that steep, smooth wall back up holding a dog, and now there were two of them trapped at the waterline. A woman named Marianna was filming from the far bank.

Up at the railing, strangers start lowering themselves down, hand in hand.

One of the men, Birzhan Seidakhmet, said that first chain just couldn't reach. So more people came off the railing, and Seidakhmet, who says he was the tallest, was lowered further to close the gap.

At the time, nobody knew who those men were, and what I love is that none of them had to go over that fence. In March 2026, a sculpture of the chain called Unity was unveiled on the Sairan embankment, and the last figure's hand reaches past the railing for anyone to take.

## CASE 13 — THE MAN BESIDE THE UPTURNED KAYAK

A small fishing boat is heading out off the coast of Western Australia on a bright morning in 2024, a GoPro bolted to the rail. The Fish Tucker Man crew are out to catch and cook their dinner.

He's worried about his bag, because his key is in it. "You're more important," the skipper tells him, and I think that's exactly the right answer.

## CASE 14 — THE SAILBOAT THROWN INTO REDONDO BEACH PIER

On March 30, 2017, just after seven in the evening, a 24-foot sailboat left King Harbor in Redondo Beach, California, with four people aboard, all in life jackets. The wind was howling, and the sea was white with chop.

They couldn't get back behind the breakwater. Every gust shoved the boat sideways toward the pier, where people were gathering at the railing, and one of them, Jason Wells, stopped his electric unicycle and started filming.

From up there, it's easy to see where this ends long before the crew can do anything about it. They're fighting the sail as the boat slides in under the outer pilings, and for a second, it looks like they'll scrape past.

Then a bigger wave stands up behind them.

The impact shook the pier, Wells said, and he was almost on top of it. Now there are four heads in the white water between the wooden pilings and the rocks, and everyone at the railing is yelling at them to swim for shore.

One by one, the sailors made it in to the rocks, where lifeguards and firefighters were arriving. The captain and another man went to the hospital with a dislocated shoulder and back pain, and the other two were treated on the beach for cuts. Fire Division Chief Rob Rappaport said they were lucky to be alive, and I don't think anyone on that pier would argue. As for the boat, it broke apart on those pilings, and it didn't even turn up in the first search.

## CASE 15 — THE BEAR THAT FOLLOWED TWO JOGGERS

Bruce Allan and Greg Armour worked together at Syncrude, in the oil sands outside Fort McMurray, Alberta. Just after eight on the morning of June 5, 2014, they were about two and a half miles into a run on the Matcheetawin trails when a young black bear stepped onto the gravel ahead.

I can't think of a worse time to meet one around there. Just over a month earlier, a black bear had killed a Suncor worker named Lorna Weafer at an oil sands site north of the city.

This one didn't leave. It followed them, Allan said, for close to 20 minutes, and he filmed the last few. Listen to how flat he keeps his voice.

They kept moving, and so did the bear. At a hairpin bend, it cut through the trees and came out ahead of them, pinning them against the tree line.

They yelled and made themselves big, and it only backed off a little. What finally got them out of there was a small tree, because the bear got distracted and started climbing it.

They ran the rest of the trail, over a little footbridge to the Jeep, and nobody was hurt. Mike Ewald, a problem wildlife specialist with Alberta Fish and Wildlife, said it was likely a young bear, possibly one people had fed before, and it wasn't showing aggression. They'd handled it well, he said, but should've carried bear spray, and both men have bought some since.

## CASE 16 — THE DOCK UNDER LAKE CHAMPLAIN

The Bassmaster Elite tournament on Lake Champlain was running a day late, because a storm had torn across the lake the day before. It was August 10, 2024, and Wes Logan, a pro from Springville, Alabama, had four bass in the boat and wanted a fifth.

Around a quarter to nine, he was running across the middle of the lake at about 50 miles an hour, his deck camera rolling. The sky's clear now, but the water's still choppy from the storm.

## CASE 17 — THE WAVE OVER THE WALL AT GIJÓN

Gijón sits on the north coast of Spain, and above its San Lorenzo beach runs a long promenade on top of a high sea wall. On Sunday, February 2, 2014, a huge swell storm was hitting that whole coast, with waves of up to 11 meters, about 36 feet, and it landed on the spring high tides.

## CASE 18 — THE TRAWLER TORN OPEN BY THE ICE

The Kapitan Bolsunovsky was a Russian freezer trawler, a factory ship that freezes its catch on board, and in May 2012 she'd been fishing the Bering Sea off Cape Navarin with 91 men aboard, in water packed with broken ice.

She wasn't cleared to be there. Ships that work in ice carry an ice class, a rating that says the hull's been strengthened for it, and at her last inspection the Bolsunovsky lost hers. Her captain knew it, and he didn't stop fishing in heavy ice.

On the morning of May 26, the ice won. Floes grinding against the hull split the welded seams on her left side, below the waterline, and the crew pulled on survival suits while a few of them kept filming.

Seawater poured into the holds, and when a watertight door between them gave way, she started to lean. The thing I notice most is how calm the sea looks. It's flat and white with ice, and a ship this size is rolling over in the middle of it.

She went down about 45 minutes after the ice broke through.

Every one of the 91 men survived. A trawler from the same company, the Dersu Uzala, wasn't far away, and it took them all aboard. So what happened to the man who'd taken her into the ice? The captain pleaded guilty, and in November 2012 a Russian court convicted him of breaking maritime safety rules. The owner's losses ran past 166 million rubles, and his fine was 140,000, roughly 4,500 dollars. That's not even a tenth of one percent of what the owner lost. The Bolsunovsky's still down there, about 660 feet deep.

## CASE 19 — THE BOOBY-TRAPPED STAIRCASE

## CASE 20 — THE BEAR ON THE KNIFE-EDGE RIDGE

A climber in his forties was alone on Mount Futago, west of Tokyo, on October 1, 2022, and it was his third time on the route. Just before nine, he was down-climbing the rocky ridge below the west peak, with long drops on both sides and a GoPro on his head. His bear bell rattles nonstop on rock, so he'd silenced it for the descent.

About 160 feet below the summit, he heard heavy, animal snorting from above. He turned around.

A full-grown black bear was coming straight down the rock at him. Where do you go on a ridge like that? Nowhere, because one wrong step means a fall. He twisted away from the first lunge, and the bear slid past him, then scrambled back up and came at him again from below.

He said that instead of fear, he switched to the feeling that if it was coming, he had no choice but to face it.

He screamed the whole time and brought his fist down on it like a hammer. Then came a front kick, and about 20 seconds after it started, the bear crashed off into the bushes below. I don't think I'd have kept my footing up there, let alone hit back. Going back through the footage, he decided it'd been a mother with a cub nearby.

He says he came away with scraped, cut hands from gripping the rock and a slightly sprained wrist, both minor. Rather than carry on down toward where the bear had gone, he climbed back up to the summit, took a breather and went down the way he'd come.

## CASE 21 — HELD AT GUNPOINT AT THE TREATCO PLANT

On April 2, 2021, urban explorer Jared Coker squeezed into the old TreatCo plant in Wichita, a pet-treat factory gutted by fires. For almost two hours it's an ordinary video of dark production floors.

## CASE 23 — THE DUCK HUNTER'S WADERS

## CASE 24 — THE VAN DOOR AT 2:30 A.M.

## CASE 25 — BRICKS THROUGH THE MOTORHOME WINDOW
