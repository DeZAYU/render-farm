## CASE 01 — GUDAURI CHAIRLIFT RUNS BACKWARDS

On March 16, 2018, it was a clear, bright day at Gudauri, a ski resort high in the mountains of Georgia, about 75 miles north of the capital, Tbilisi. A long line of skiers was waiting at the bottom of a chairlift, and someone at the base station had a phone out, filming. Nothing about the queue looked wrong. Then the chairs coming down the mountain started moving faster than they should.

The chairs that should be climbing the mountain are coming back down it, and they're picking up speed. This is Sadzele, the resort's highest chairlift, with its top station at about 10,760 feet. It had lost power and stopped, then started moving again in the wrong direction. It's running backwards at about twice its normal speed, with people still sitting in the seats. And at the base station, there's nowhere for them to go.

Some riders were thrown off, and others jumped into the snow before they reached the big wheel. The queue scattered, and then people ran straight back in to help.

Nobody died. Eleven people were hurt, among them tourists from Sweden and Ukraine. A pregnant Swedish woman was airlifted to a hospital in Tbilisi, and her injuries weren't life threatening. All of the injured were later invited back to ski the next season for free.

A week later, Georgia's economy minister gave the result of a review by the testing company Bureau Veritas. The lift had been inspected just three months earlier and declared in perfect working order. The power cut had stopped it, but the rollback itself was human error. The operator was supposed to switch to the backup diesel drive and bring everyone down safely, and he didn't follow that procedure. The Interior Ministry opened a criminal case, but no outcome was ever made public. Two senior managers resigned, and the operator was fired.

## CASE 02 — STAGE UNDER A BLACK SKY AT THE INDIANA STATE FAIR

The grandstand at the Indiana State Fair in Indianapolis was packed on the night of August 13, 2011, for the country duo Sugarland. Their show was due to start at 8:45. Fans had pushed right up to the front of the stage, under a temporary roof. That roof was a steel truss of lights and speakers weighing about 70,000 pounds, strapped down to concrete road barriers.

To the west, the sky was going black. At 8 p.m., the fair's executive director, Cindy Hoye, wanted to delay the show. Sugarland's side preferred to go on a few minutes late, and she agreed. Around 8:30, a state police captain named Brad Weaver recommended canceling and getting an evacuation plan ready. At 8:39, the National Weather Service warned of one-inch hail and winds over 60 miles an hour. That warning didn't reach Hoye or Weaver.

The clip you're about to see was filmed by a fan at the edge of the grandstand, and her own time stamp reads 8:45. The stage is lit up under a sky that's almost black. Then she swings the camera east, over the parking lot.

Look at how low that shelf of cloud is riding over the car lights.

An announcer told the crowd there were clouds to the west and where to go if they had to leave. He said everyone was hoping the weather would miss them, but there was a very good chance it wouldn't, and Sugarland would be out in a couple of minutes. The crowd cheered, and almost everyone stayed. The storm itself wasn't forecast to arrive until around 9:15.

The wind didn't wait. Hoye and Weaver were walking to the stage to order an evacuation when, at 8:46, a gust of about 59 miles an hour raced out ahead of the storm. The rig tipped forward onto the fans at the front. Seven people died, four of them at the scene, and 58 were injured.

That's one minute after the time stamp on the clip we just watched.

The engineering review found those barriers weren't fixed to anything, so they simply slid. They could only hold back winds of 25 to 43 miles an hour, and the code demanded 68. A second review found nobody had a clear rule for calling off a show.

The state fined the stage company, and by the end of 2014, payouts to the families and the injured had reached $50 million. No criminal charges were ever announced.

In May 2012, Indiana adopted its first rules for inspecting outdoor stage rigging. They were only emergency rules, though, and they were allowed to lapse.

## CASE 03 — WHAKAARI WHITE ISLAND TOUR BEFORE THE ERUPTION

Allessandro Kauffmann had come to New Zealand from Brazil with his partner, and on Monday, December 9, 2019, he wore a GoPro for a day trip to Whakaari, also called White Island. It's an active volcano about 30 miles off the Bay of Plenty coast, and for years tour boats had been taking visitors right onto the crater floor. Many of the people heading out that day had come ashore from the cruise ship Ovation of the Seas.

Three weeks earlier, the volcano's official alert level had gone up from 1 to 2. Tremor and gas readings were the highest they'd been since 2016. The tours didn't stop.

At first it's just a vacation video, with the wharf and the boat ride out to the island. The group goes ashore in an inflatable and everyone's handed a hard hat. Then they're walking over gray rock and yellow sulfur crust, and the steam's pouring out of vents all around them while the guide points things out.

They pass the rusted ruins of an old sulfur works and head back down to the jetty. Kauffmann's group was among the last to tour the crater that day. They climbed back onto the boat, and he said later it wasn't even five minutes from then until the volcano erupted. At 2:11 in the afternoon, a column of black ash and steam shot up out of the crater behind them, about 2.3 miles high. On the boat, a guide came over the loudspeaker and told everyone to move inside.

There'd been 47 people on the island. Boats nearby turned back and put inflatables in the water to pull people off the jetty, and three helicopter pilots landed in the ash and flew 12 survivors out. Twenty-two people died, and two of them were never found. The other 25 survived, almost all of them badly hurt.

WorkSafe New Zealand, the country's workplace safety regulator, later charged 13 parties over the tours. Six pleaded guilty, including the tour company and GNS Science, the state-owned institute that ran the volcano monitoring. The island's owners, Whakaari Management, fought their charge and were convicted after a trial in 2023. In March 2024, the court ordered more than 10 million New Zealand dollars in reparations, roughly 250,000 for each survivor and each bereaved family.

Then, in February 2025, the High Court overturned the owners' conviction. Justice Simon Moore ruled that they hadn't controlled the tour workplace, so the company that owned the island doesn't carry a conviction for any of it. Later that year a coroner began hearing evidence, and the families' lawyer told him that many of them still believe they should never have been walking in that crater.

## CASE 04 — CHAPECOENSE PLAYERS FILM INSIDE LAMIA 2933

On November 28, 2016, Chapecoense was in the best week of its history. The small club from the south of Brazil had just reached the final of the Copa Sudamericana, South America's second-biggest club competition, and the first leg was in Medellín, Colombia.

Brazil's aviation agency wouldn't approve the charter airline LaMia for the whole trip, so the team boarded it in Santa Cruz, Bolivia. Flight 2933 was an Avro RJ85 that had flown Argentina's national team, Messi included, less than three weeks earlier. Seventy-seven people were on board, 22 of them players. Departure ran late, partly because a team member asked for a video game to be fetched from the cargo hold.

This clip was shared as one player's video from on board that night. He turns the phone on himself, and the rows behind him are full of grinning teammates in club tracksuits. Nobody in that cabin is worried about anything except the final.

What none of them knew about was the fuel. The flight to Medellín was about 1,840 miles, close to the plane's maximum range. There'd been a refueling stop planned at Cobija, but with the late start that airport would be closed before they arrived, so they flew straight on.

International rules called for about 12 metric tons of fuel on a trip like that. The plane carried just over 9, and the trip itself needed almost all of it. To me, roughly 215 kilograms of spare fuel isn't a margin at all.

Around 9:16 p.m., a low fuel warning came on. The crew didn't divert, and they didn't tell air traffic control. Near Medellín they were told to hold for another plane. At 9:52 they finally declared an emergency. A minute later two engines stopped, and the other two followed two minutes after that. At 9:59, after the pilot radioed that he had no fuel and no electrics, the plane came down on a mountain called Cerro Gordo, short of the airport.

Seventy-one people died, and six survived. A surviving technician, Erwin Tumiri, said there was never an emergency announcement. He'd thought they were just descending.

Colombia's investigators blamed inadequate fuel planning by the airline, plus poor decisions by the crew. The pilot, Miguel Quiroga, was also one of LaMia's co-owners. The cockpit voice recorder had stopped about an hour and forty minutes before the end, and investigators thought he'd most likely pulled its circuit breaker, though they couldn't prove it. The airline's insurance had lapsed that October for non-payment.

Atlético Nacional, the team Chapecoense should've played, asked for the title to go to Chapecoense, and it did. Eight months later, defender Alan Ruschel, one of the survivors, walked out at the Camp Nou as Chapecoense's captain.

## CASE 05 — AYALA CENTER CEBU CINEMA 5 CEILING COLLAPSE

Say you're sitting in Cinema 5 at Ayala Center Cebu on a Monday night in June 2015. Your company, Dreamscape Networks, has rented the whole place for a launch party, with about 300 people in the seats.

Someone at the back is filming, and you can already see the problem. Water's streaming from the ceiling above the screen onto the front rows. Above them, the water had been collecting on the acoustic boards hung under the real roof. Dreamscape's CEO later said there were sparks and a bang first. Still, nobody runs. People shuffle sideways and stand up to look.

I'd like to think I'd have moved. I'm not sure I would've.

The panels came down at about 8:50. Nine people were hurt and five went to the hospital. Nobody died.

The mall blamed the sprinkler system. The city's building official later said inspectors found a loosened joint in the sprinkler line, possibly shaken loose by an earthquake that March. The CEO claimed mall security ran away and that proper help took 25 minutes. Police said they only heard about it at 9:10, from someone in the media. The mall apologized, and Cebu's mayor ordered every cinema in the city checked.

## CASE 06 — ÇÖPLER MINE LANDSLIDE

Above the district of İliç, in the dry hills of eastern Turkey, there's a gold mine that doesn't have a single tunnel. The Çöpler mine is run by a company called Anagold, and its centerpiece is a mountain somebody built. Crushed ore gets piled up in terraces and soaked with a cyanide solution that slowly pulls the gold out. It's called a heap-leach pad, and this one towered over the valley.

Around nine on the morning of February 13, 2024, workers doing a routine check spotted cracks running across the heap. They reported it, and the area was cleared.

So why were nine people still up there that afternoon? According to Turkey's energy minister, three supervisors had driven back up to check on the cracks. Five miners were waiting in a site container nearby, and a truck driver was out on his route.

At 2:28 p.m., a worker across the valley was filming from behind a low stone wall, with a yellow bulldozer blade in the foreground. We don't know who it was. Watch the far slope, because it doesn't come down the way you'd expect a hillside to.

It flows. Roughly ten million cubic meters of cyanide-treated soil and ore, about 400,000 dump-truck loads, poured down a slope of around 650 feet and ran nearly half a mile toward the stream below. All nine workers were buried under it, and all nine were killed.

More than 800 rescuers searched with radar and dogs. On February 19 they had to stop, because the rest of the heap looked ready to come down on them. The search resumed, but the first of the nine wasn't found for 53 days. The last were recovered in June.

Prosecutors say the heap was about twice the size it should've been, and scientists measured what was left at around 840 feet against a safe limit of roughly 490. More solution had been pumped into it, too. What I can't get past is the blasting nearby every day at noon, which prosecutors list among the causes.

Within four days, Turkey canceled the mine's license and its environmental permits. Twelve people were arrested pending trial, including the company's Canadian manager, and 43 defendants went on trial in March 2025. By October 2026 nobody was still held in pretrial detention, and nobody had been convicted. The next hearing is set for January 26, 2027.

## CASE 07 — EDOUARD BOZON CREVASSE

Edouard Bozon is a skier from Lyon, and in the spring of 2020 he was high above Chamonix with a partner and a helmet camera running. They'd roped down a steep rock section, and now they were skiing a narrow couloir off the Capucin glacier with no rope between them.

At the bottom of the couloir, there's a deep crack where the snow meets the glacier, called a bergschrund. Edouard stops right beside it to wait, while his partner's still on the slope above him.

His partner had shifted his weight up there, and a small slab of snow broke loose and swept Edouard over the edge. He's wedged inside with his skis jammed and a small hole of daylight overhead. And what's one of the first things he says down there? Warn the others, because anyone still coming down could knock more snow in on him.

His partner found the hole fast, but he didn't have a rope. He lowered a strap, then his ice axe. Edouard was scared that unclipping his skis would drop him deeper, but he finally got both off. He pulled himself up far enough for his partner to haul him out.

A rescue helicopter flew him off the mountain with a badly hurt knee. The next March, he posted a new film of himself skiing steep lines again.

## CASE 08 — SANTIKA CLUB

This clip was shot from the floor of Santika, a famous club on Ekkamai in Bangkok, at 11:44 p.m. on December 31, 2008. The man holding the camera is Scott Garpot, an American who'd come with his brother. The party's called Goodbye Santika, the last New Year's blowout before the club moves to a new home, and about a thousand guests and staff are packed inside.

On stage, a band called Burn is playing, and at the front of the stage, fountains of sparks shoot up in time with the music. Then Garpot tilts up to the balcony, and they're shoulder to shoulder up there, waiting for a countdown that's about fifteen minutes away.

Nobody in that room knew what the building was on paper. Santika wasn't licensed as a nightclub at all. Officials later found it'd been registered as a food business that had to close at midnight, and as a private home. It hadn't once been inspected for fire. Even the city architect's signature on its plans turned out to be forged.

So how many ways out did a thousand people have? One main front door, about eight feet wide, plus a staff exit. A third exit had been locked to keep thieves out. There was one fire extinguisher.

Sometime after midnight on January 1, 2009, the ceiling above the stage caught fire. Garpot wrote that it'd spread across the ceiling in less than four minutes. Then the lights went out and part of the ceiling dome came down. Hundreds of people pushed toward the one door they knew, and it jammed solid.

Sixty-six people died and 222 were hurt, with citizens of 13 countries among them. Garpot's brother was one of the injured, and he was still in intensive care when Garpot posted this clip two days later. Garpot wrote that he'd dropped his own camera while helping other people get out.

Police first pointed at the singer and the stage effects. The Justice Ministry's forensic experts later cleared him and said a stage effect wired into the electrics was the likely source. The cause hasn't ever been officially settled. All anyone can say for sure is that it started on that stage.

In 2011 the owner and the pyrotechnics contractor got three years each for negligence. After an appeal went the other way, Thailand's Supreme Court gave the owner three years again in 2015. Another court ruled that Bangkok's city government shared the blame for letting the place stay open.

The spot on Ekkamai where Santika stood has since been rebuilt as a lifestyle mall.

## CASE 09 — PUKKELPOP FROM THE DJ BOOTH

On August 18, 2011, the opening day of Pukkelpop, about 60,000 people were spread across the festival grounds at Kiewit, near Hasselt in Belgium. Inside one of the big tents, the American rapper Wiz Khalifa was on his last song, and behind him his tour DJ, DJ Bonics, had a phone out over the edge of the booth. It's a great shot. Thousands of people are lit up orange with their hands in the air, packed all the way to the back.

A code orange thunderstorm warning was in force that evening. At a festival, though, a storm usually means you get wet and that's about it. Then Bonics turns the phone around, toward the open doors behind the stage.

What was happening overhead doesn't happen often. A line of storms had merged into a bow shape, and just after six it dropped a burst of air straight down onto the site. Scientists later worked out that this downburst was less than about 330 feet across and lasted only minutes, with gusts estimated at somewhere between 65 and 83 miles an hour.

One thing about the tent we're watching. It's been passed around as the one that came down, and it wasn't. It's very probably the Dance Hall, and it shook but held, with none of the victims inside.

Other parts of the site weren't so lucky. Trees were torn out, and light towers and screens fell. The Chateau tent came down while a band was playing. Part of a structure collapsed at the Boiler Room, and out on the campsite, scaffolding fell onto tents where people had gone to shelter. Five people died, and about 140 were hurt. The rest of the festival was canceled the next day.

When Bonics walks outside at the end of his clip, the field is a lake and people are wading through it. Posting the video the next day, he called it a madhouse.

Prosecutors in Hasselt concluded it was force majeure, an event nobody could have prevented, and found no fault by the organizers. Belgium was also forecasting half blind that day. Only one of its weather radars was working, about 75 miles away.

Pukkelpop now has its tents anchor-tested and inspected before the gates open. And today almost every festival in Belgium has its own meteorologist on site.

## CASE 10 — KERN RIVER CANYON HEAD-ON

The stamp on this dashcam reads September 20, 2020, 11:09 in the morning. This is Highway 178 in California's Kern River Canyon, a two-lane road squeezed between rock and river, and the double yellow line runs unbroken because you rarely see what's coming around the bend. For ten seconds, the camera just follows the traffic. Then a dark sedan comes up on the left to pass everyone on a curve.

A pickup was coming the other way, and the two met head-on right in front of the camera. Whoever posted the clip three days later wrote that no fatalities were reported, and no police report of the crash was ever made public. On a road where the Highway Patrol counted 20 deaths in five years, I'd call that luck.

## CASE 11 — A CORUÑA LAUNDROMAT

A man in a dark jacket is collecting his clean laundry at a self-service laundromat in A Coruña, in northwest Spain. It's March 2023, and a ceiling camera looks down on a row of gray chairs and a big industrial dryer right by the glass door. He picks up his bags and walks out through that door. Count along with me once it swings shut.

That's about four seconds before the dryer bursts open. The blast went straight out through the door he'd just used and took the shop window with it. Had he lingered, he'd have been standing right in front of it. Nobody was hurt, and the damage stayed with the shop.

Firefighters in A Coruña found the cause inside the machine. Someone had left a refill cartridge for a gas lighter in their clothes, and it burst in the heat of the drum.

## CASE 12 — VAASA ARENA DUCT

Vaasa Arena is the ice hall in Vaasa, on Finland's west coast, and on December 17, 2017, about 3,000 people packed into it for Raskasta Joulua, or Heavy Christmas. That's a touring show where metal singers belt out Christmas carols. Sonata Arctica's Tony Kakko was singing, and a fan up in the stands was filming the stage.

The show had already used fake snow and other effects. So when the stage pyro died away after the third song, most people thought nothing of the rattling that started up in the roof, right above the stage.

That was a piece of the hall's exhaust air duct, at least three feet wide, coming down with a lighting rail. Kakko first thought it was another effect, then shouted "Watch out" into the mic. It's about 480 pounds of metal, and it dropped more than 30 feet into the standing crowd, though electrical cables slowed it down.

There was no stampede. One witness said nobody was panicking, and everyone was just bewildered. A doctor or nurse in the crowd started first aid before the ambulances got there. Investigators said about twenty people were hurt and nobody died, but they warned that the pieces coming down edge first could've killed someone.

Finland's Safety Investigation Authority found the duct hadn't been hung properly. One of its brackets had already failed a few days earlier, and that put the extra weight on the other hangers until they couldn't hold it.

Five men were convicted of negligent bodily injury, including the CEO of the company that'd modified and fastened the duct. By the end of 2021, the Vaasa Court of Appeal had upheld their verdicts.

## CASE 13 — RUSSIAN TRUCK WINDSCREEN

## CASE 14 — NORTH PERRY CESSNA

A student pilot lines up for runway 10 at North Perry Airport in Pembroke Pines, Florida, on a clear Tuesday morning, November 12, 2019. He's flying solo in a Cessna 172, with a camera clipped behind the seats watching over his shoulder.

The plane was built in 1978, so it was 41 years old that day. For about twenty seconds the approach looks textbook, with his hands steady on the yoke and the runway numbers sliding closer. He'd later tell investigators his airspeed was slightly high.

Keep an eye on the nose right after the wheels touch.

Right rudder and right aileron didn't stop the swing to the left. The plane ran off into the grass and across a taxiway. It hit a taxiway sign before it tipped onto its left side.

He climbed out on his own with minor injuries at most, and he later went back to training. Fire crews cleaned up a small fuel leak, and the runway was closed for a while.

With a plane that old, I'd have guessed something broke. But he reported no mechanical failure, and the National Transportation Safety Board's report doesn't point to one either. His flight school said he should've aborted the landing and gone around. The board's probable cause was the student's failure to keep directional control during the landing.

## CASE 15 — IRAN SINGING SELFIE CRASH

Two women in Tehran were filming themselves in the front of a car one night in May 2014, singing along to a pop song called "Ghorboni". You never see the road. Reports said the driver let go of the wheel to make a heart with her hands, and the heart comes up about five seconds in.

About fourteen seconds in, there's a bright flash and the picture whites out. The car had crashed head-on.

They posted the clip anyway, and within days it was everywhere. Nobody's independently confirmed it's genuine, but the police treated it as real.

On May 18, the head of accidents at Tehran's traffic police said the crash had never been reported to them. Even so, he said both women had come out of it unhurt, and that they'd broken the law and would be dealt with. Other reports said they were treated in the hospital for minor injuries.

## CASE 16 — TYSON PEERS DERECHO SHELTER

The hut in this video is a turf-roofed shelter that Tyson Peers built by hand for his bushcraft channel, in the woods near his home in Hawkesbury, Ontario. On the afternoon of Saturday, May 21, 2022, he was hauling rocks for its chimney, and his brother and a friend were helping out.

Then his phone goes off. The screen reads 4:27, and it's an emergency alert for a dangerous thunderstorm. The other two want to run for the house. Tyson tells them there isn't time, and that out in the open, with trees all around, is the worst place they could be. He gets everyone into the hut and holds onto them.

From here the camera's inside in the dark, and the doorway's your only window.

Two trees came down right on top of the hut, and the roof held. It's all over in minutes, and when they crawl out, the path home's buried under snapped trunks. Tyson said afterward that staying put "turned out to be the best decision because we would have died." Looking at that path, I don't doubt it for a second.

What hit them was a derecho, a fast-moving line of storms. This one ran about 600 miles across Ontario and Quebec in roughly nine hours, and its strongest measured gust, in London, Ontario, was about 103 miles an hour. More than 1.1 million customers lost power, and it's one of the costliest natural disasters Canada's ever had. At least 12 people were killed, most of them by falling trees.

## CASE 17 — MALKAPUR DEMOLITION

Malkapur's big lake sits about 37 miles from Hyderabad, in southern India, and in September 2024 a four-story concrete building stood out in its water, inside the official flood line. It'd been a family's weekend house for about twelve years, until villagers complained.

No vehicle could reach it across the lake, so on September 26, local officials blew it up. Reportedly, it was the first time they'd used explosives to clear a building like this. A crowd's watching from a rooftop that looks like a safe distance away. Focus on the base of the tower.

A slab of concrete made it all the way to the roof. It hit a home guard named Gopal and knocked him unconscious, and a second person was hurt too. Officials said every precaution had been taken. Gopal was declared out of danger in a Hyderabad hospital, but days later his family said he still couldn't speak properly. His doctors said he'd need at least four months of speech therapy.

## CASE 19 — THE SKYE LIFE DAM SIRENS

## CASE 20 — CAPE TOWN N2 OFFICER

On November 17, 2015, in the morning rush on Cape Town's N2 highway, traffic officer Nizaam Alexander pulled over a silver Mercedes that was driving in the yellow emergency lane. His patrol car's dashcam kept rolling. A city safety official said lots of their officers run cameras because drivers lie about what happened.

He's at the driver's window doing the paperwork. While he's there, check the grass verge on the left of the frame.

The man in the dark hoodie had come up behind him and tried to take his service weapon, and when Alexander reacted, he was shot in the back. His vest stopped anything serious. Alexander rolled clear and chased the attacker on foot toward the shacks beside the highway, but the man got away into the informal settlement.

The Western Cape Government released the footage in January 2016 and asked the public for help. Officials said people recognized a man from that grainy footage, and a suspect was arrested in early February 2016. No trial or verdict has ever been made public.

What people remember, though, is what Alexander did that morning. He walked back to the Mercedes and finished issuing the fine for the yellow lane. He didn't go for medical help until he was done. You can see the hole in the back of his uniform while he writes.

Who does that? Someone who'd just won the City's departmental Traffic Officer of the Year award for 2015. He got that award before this morning, not because of it.
