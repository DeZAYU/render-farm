On November 28, 2016, Chapecoense was in the best week of its history. The small club from the south of Brazil had just reached the final of the Copa Sudamericana, South America's second-biggest club competition, and the first leg was in Medellín, Colombia.

Brazil's aviation agency wouldn't approve the charter airline LaMia for the whole trip, so the team boarded it in Santa Cruz, Bolivia. Flight 2933 was an Avro RJ85 that had flown Argentina's national team, Messi included, less than three weeks earlier. Seventy-seven people were on board, 22 of them players. Departure ran late, partly because a team member asked for a video game to be fetched from the cargo hold.

This clip was shared as one player's video from on board that night. He turns the phone on himself, and the rows behind him are full of grinning teammates in club tracksuits. Nobody in that cabin is worried about anything except the final.

What none of them knew about was the fuel. The flight to Medellín was about 1,840 miles, close to the plane's maximum range. There'd been a refueling stop planned at Cobija, but with the late start that airport would be closed before they arrived, so they flew straight on.

International rules called for about 12 metric tons of fuel on a trip like that. The plane carried just over 9, and the trip itself needed almost all of it. To me, roughly 215 kilograms of spare fuel isn't a margin at all.

Around 9:16 p.m., a low fuel warning came on. The crew didn't divert, and they didn't tell air traffic control. Near Medellín they were told to hold for another plane. At 9:52 they finally declared an emergency. A minute later two engines stopped, and the other two followed two minutes after that. At 9:59, after the pilot radioed that he had no fuel and no electrics, the plane came down on a mountain called Cerro Gordo, short of the airport.

Seventy-one people died, and six survived. A surviving technician, Erwin Tumiri, said there was never an emergency announcement. He'd thought they were just descending.

Colombia's investigators blamed inadequate fuel planning by the airline, plus poor decisions by the crew. The pilot, Miguel Quiroga, was also one of LaMia's co-owners. The cockpit voice recorder had stopped about an hour and forty minutes before the end, and investigators thought he'd most likely pulled its circuit breaker, though they couldn't prove it. The airline's insurance had lapsed that October for non-payment.

Atlético Nacional, the team Chapecoense should've played, asked for the title to go to Chapecoense, and it did. Eight months later, defender Alan Ruschel, one of the survivors, walked out at the Camp Nou as Chapecoense's captain.
