The grandstand at the Indiana State Fair in Indianapolis was packed on the night of August 13, 2011, for the country duo Sugarland. Their show was due to start at 8:45. Fans had pushed right up to the front of the stage, under a temporary roof. That roof was a steel truss of lights and speakers weighing about 70,000 pounds, strapped down to concrete road barriers.

To the west, the sky was going black. At 8 p.m., the fair's executive director, Cindy Hoye, wanted to delay the show. Sugarland's side preferred to go on a few minutes late, and she agreed. Around 8:30, a state police captain named Brad Weaver recommended canceling and getting an evacuation plan ready. At 8:39, the National Weather Service warned of one-inch hail and winds over 60 miles an hour. That warning didn't reach Hoye or Weaver.

The clip you're about to see was filmed by a fan at the edge of the grandstand, and her own time stamp reads 8:45. The stage is lit up under a sky that's almost black. Then she swings the camera east, over the parking lot.

Look at how low that shelf of cloud is riding over the car lights.

An announcer told the crowd there were clouds to the west and where to go if they had to leave. He said everyone was hoping the weather would miss them, but there was a very good chance it wouldn't, and Sugarland would be out in a couple of minutes. The crowd cheered, and almost everyone stayed. The storm itself wasn't forecast to arrive until around 9:15.

The wind didn't wait. Hoye and Weaver were walking to the stage to order an evacuation when, at 8:46, a gust of about 59 miles an hour raced out ahead of the storm. The rig tipped forward onto the fans at the front. Seven people died, four of them at the scene, and 58 were injured.

That's one minute after the time stamp on the clip we just watched.

The engineering review found those barriers weren't fixed to anything, so they simply slid. They could only hold back winds of 25 to 43 miles an hour, and the code demanded 68. A second review found nobody had a clear rule for calling off a show.

The state fined the stage company, and by the end of 2014, payouts to the families and the injured had reached $50 million. No criminal charges were ever announced.

In May 2012, Indiana adopted its first rules for inspecting outdoor stage rigging. They were only emergency rules, though, and they were allowed to lapse.
