On August 18, 2011, the opening day of Pukkelpop, about 60,000 people were spread across the festival grounds at Kiewit, near Hasselt in Belgium. Inside one of the big tents, the American rapper Wiz Khalifa was on his last song, and behind him his tour DJ, DJ Bonics, had a phone out over the edge of the booth. It's a great shot. Thousands of people are lit up orange with their hands in the air, packed all the way to the back.

A code orange thunderstorm warning was in force that evening. At a festival, though, a storm usually means you get wet and that's about it. Then Bonics turns the phone around, toward the open doors behind the stage.

What was happening overhead doesn't happen often. A line of storms had merged into a bow shape, and just after six it dropped a burst of air straight down onto the site. Scientists later worked out that this downburst was less than about 330 feet across and lasted only minutes, with gusts estimated at somewhere between 65 and 83 miles an hour.

One thing about the tent we're watching. It's been passed around as the one that came down, and it wasn't. It's very probably the Dance Hall, and it shook but held, with none of the victims inside.

Other parts of the site weren't so lucky. Trees were torn out, and light towers and screens fell. The Chateau tent came down while a band was playing. Part of a structure collapsed at the Boiler Room, and out on the campsite, scaffolding fell onto tents where people had gone to shelter. Five people died, and about 140 were hurt. The rest of the festival was canceled the next day.

When Bonics walks outside at the end of his clip, the field is a lake and people are wading through it. Posting the video the next day, he called it a madhouse.

Prosecutors in Hasselt concluded it was force majeure, an event nobody could have prevented, and found no fault by the organizers. Belgium was also forecasting half blind that day. Only one of its weather radars was working, about 75 miles away.

Pukkelpop now has its tents anchor-tested and inspected before the gates open. And today almost every festival in Belgium has its own meteorologist on site.
